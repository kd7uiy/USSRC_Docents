Quick Plane:
•
The Quick Plane was designed and built by William Lafayette Quick, a native of New 
Market, AL. 
•
Quick was a contemporary of the Wright brothers and was intrigued by the idea of 
human flight.  
•
He studied birds and insects, noting the structure of their wings and how they glide.  His 
observations aided him in the design of his flying machine.  
•
He completed the plane in 1908, only five years after the Wright Brother’s historic flight 
at Kitty Hawk.  
•
The Quick Plane had a couple of different components than the Wright brothers’ plane.  
•
Firstly, it enabled the pilot to sit upright in the plane.  
•
Additionally, it was a monoplane, meaning it had one wing on each side.  The 
Wright brothers’ plane was a biplane, having two wings on each side. 
•
The Quick Plane flew one flight, and sustained damage to the landing gear and right wing 
upon touchdown.  
•
In 1912, Quick applied for a patent for an “Improved Flying Machine”. This spiritual 
successor to his original design had two components that would prove to be integral 
parts of later planes—the retractable landing gear and the steering tail wheel, later 
replaced by nose wheel steering.  
•
Only 60 years separate the flight of the Quick Plane and the first manned flight of the 
Saturn V rocket (December 1968 – Apollo 8).

V2:
•
The V2 was developed and used during World War II by Germany.  
•
Wernher von Braun and his rocket team developed the Aggregate series of rockets 
initially in Kummersdorf, with later work at Peenemunde.  
•
The A4 is what today is commonly called the V2 (Hitler called his rockets “vengeance 
weapons,” giving them a V instead of an A).  
•
These rockets were propelled by an ethanol and water propellant and liquid oxygen.  
•
Germany began launching the V2s in 1944, specifically to target London and 
Antwerp.  
•
Over 1000 V2s were launched during the war.  
•
It was the best technology of the era and coveted by all—they could…
•
fly 200 miles
•
carry a 2200-pound warhead
•
reach a height of 55 miles when launched for long range (higher for short 
range)
•
travel at a max speed of 3580 mph, and impacted at 1790 mph.

Operation Paperclip:
As the war drew to a close, von Braun and his team recognized that Germany would 
not win, and they had few options.  
As a team, they decided to surrender to the U.S., as they would be treated better by 
the Americans than they would by the Soviets, and that the U.S. would have the 
means to continue working on rockets and perhaps give the team the ability to 
continue their first desire: to build rockets for space travel.  
The German rocket scientists and their families were brought to the U.S. through a 
secret initiative known as Operation Paperclip.  Paperclips were placed on the files of 
German rocket scientists who were being brought to the U.S.
Von Braun’s brother Magnus spoke the best English, so he was sent out on a bicycle 
to the nearest American check point to relay that there were rocket scientists in the 
area ready to surrender.  
The specifics were worked out, and the team was interviewed by the US to determine 
that they were indeed rocket scientists.  Over 100 scientists from that team and 300 
railcars of V2 components were sent to White Sands, NM, to help train and educate 
American rocket scientists.  More joined them later, and this is where the American 
space program really began moving forward.

The Mercury Program
Before 1958, there was little information on the space environment.  No one knew if 
humans could function in space, or what they would need to take with them in order 
to survive.  Between 1958 and 1963, both manned and unmanned missions were 
launched to find the answers to these questions. Project Mercury included the first 
manned U.S. missions into space.  Seven astronauts were selected for this first project.  
The exhibit in the SVH is a trainer that was used at the Langley Research Center in 
Langley, VA.  Astronauts practiced all their procedures for operating the capsule in this 
trainer.  Technicians would insert anomalies or problems that they anticipated in flight 
to train the astronauts for a variety of in-flight situations.
Goals for Project Mercury:
1.
Put a man and craft into Earth orbit
2.
Investigate man’s ability to work in space
3.
Recover both astronaut and spacecraft safely
The capsules were being built before astronauts were recruited.  Many men applying 
to be astronauts were not able to fit into the capsule.  Based on the measurements of 
the capsule, it was determined that astronauts could not be taller than 5’11”.
The space race was part of the Cold War between the U.S. and the Soviet Union 
(USSR).  
•
The USSR beat the U.S. in launching the first satellite to space – called Sputnik - in 
1957
•
The USSR also launched the first living thing into orbit - a dog named Laika.  
•
The Soviet’s first manned space program was called Vostok and was similar to the 
Mercury program.  
•
Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin was the first man in space and the first man in 
orbit, launched on April 12, 1961 from Baikonur, Kazakhstan.  
•
The Soviet achievements in their space program served as a motivating factor for 
the acceleration of the U.S. space program.

Mercury Launch Vehicles
Two different rockets were used to launch the Mercury capsules into space. 
•
Initially, the Mercury Redstone was used. 
•
This was a variation on the Redstone missile, which was the first missile 
that Dr. von Braun had built for the United States. 
•
The Mercury Redstone was nicknamed “Old Reliable” because of its 
excellent service record. 
•
Being a single stage missile, it lacked the power for orbital flight. 
•
Alan Shepard and Gus Grissom both rode the Mercury Redstone up into 
space and straight back down to Earth (about 15-minute flights). 
•
NASA realized that it needed a more powerful vehicle to achieve orbit. They 
switched to the Mercury Atlas, another modified missile. 
•
The Mercury Atlas was an early multi-stage design (not quite 2, but 1 ½). 
•
The extra half-stage provided the extra power which was necessary to 
achieve orbit around the Earth. 
•
John Glenn would be the first American in orbit
•
The rest of the Mercury astronauts would also use the Atlas to successfully 
reach the Earth’s orbit.

The Mercury 7, in order of flights:
(from left to right: Scott Carpenter, Gordon Cooper, John Glenn, Gus Grissom, Wally Schirra, Alan Shepard, Deke Slayton)
Alan Shepard -
•
Alan Shepard was the first American in space.  
•
His capsule, Freedom 7, was launched by a Mercury Redstone launch vehicle on 
May 5, 1961.  
•
The flight was 15 minutes and suborbital.
•
Story: Shepard had a problem on that first flight that no one had foreseen. He drank 
a lot of coffee for breakfast the morning before his launch, on the way to the Cape, 
and during meetings leading up to the launch.  Once he was secured in the capsule, 
he realized that he had to go—to the bathroom.  
•
He reported this to the CAPCOM (another astronaut who is responsible for 
communication between the capsule and mission control). Shepard’s flight was only 
supposed to be 15-18 minutes, so he was told to hold it. 
•
As it often happens with space flight, there were delays, and Shepard was on the 
launch pad for about 4 hours. He couldn’t be let out because of the time involved, 
and the people on the ground didn’t want water in the capsule—there were a lot of 
electronics and Alan himself was wired so his health could be monitored.  
•
Finally, a message came from the ground that he could go.  Shepard was quite happy 
and asked when they would be letting him out.  He was told to go in his suit—which 
he did, because there was no choice.
•
Shortly after, he was launched on his first flight.

Gus Grissom -
•
Gus Grissom was the second American in space.  
•
He was launched on a Mercury Redstone on July 21, 1961 and named his capsule 
the Liberty Bell 7 because he thought the capsule looked like a bell.  
•
The only thing missing was the crack, which he painted on as a joke.  
•
All was going well with Gus on his suborbital flight until landing.  Gus’s capsule 
splashed down in the ocean and the hatch blew open causing the capsule to take 
on water.  Gus got out, but the capsule was not recovered and sank to the bottom 
of the ocean.  There was suspicion that Gus had done something to cause the 
hatch to pop open.  He denied doing anything to cause the incident, but it was 
1999 before the capsule was retrieved and he was proven to be innocent of any 
action causing the sinking.
John Glenn –
•
John Glenn launched on a Mercury Atlas launch vehicle on February 20, 1962 and 
became the first American to orbit the Earth.  
•
His trip on the Friendship 7 took five hours and he performed three orbits before 
returning to Earth.  
•
He reported seeing fireflies while in orbit, which created a mystery.  
•
John was declared a National Treasure by the president and did not fly in space 
again until 1998 when he flew aboard the space shuttle Discovery (STS-95).  He 
was 77 years old and became the oldest man to visit space.
Scott Carpenter –
•
Scott Carpenter flew next on May 24, 1962.  
•
He solved the mystery of Glenn’s fireflies - frozen condensate on the windows and 
the play of the lights
•
While on his mission, Carpenter used too much fuel, and splashed down over 100 
miles off target. 
•
He was asked by NASA to not fly again – he never did. 
Wally Schirra –
•
Wally Schirra flew after Carpenter.
•
He was nicknamed Textbook Wally because everything on his flight went near-
perfectly, and his splash down was the closest to the target of any of the Mercury 
astronauts. 
•
Schirra would later fly in both the Gemini and Apollo programs – The only 
American astronaut to fly in all 3 early American space programs

•

Deke Slayton –
•
Deke Slayton was selected as part of the Mercury 7, but it was discovered that he

•
He was able to overcome his condition (he credits healthy eating, vitamins and 
exercise) and later flew the Apollo-Soyuz Test Program in 1975.

The Gemini Program
The New Nine
For the Gemini program, new astronauts would be needed. The second Group of 
NASA astronauts called themselves the “New Nine” or the “Next Nine”. With many of 
the original Mercury Seven astronauts either retiring, being asked to not fly again, or 
sidelined by disease, it would be up to these rookie astronauts to take on high levels 
of responsibility in the Gemini and the Apollo Programs. Seven out of the nine of 
them would go on to command Apollo Missions.

The next step in American manned space travel was Project Gemini.  Gemini was a 
stepping stone to travel to the moon.  
The number 2 comes up many times in the Gemini program.  
•
Gemini was named for the constellation Gemini – “Gemini” meaning “the twins.”
•
It was the 2nd manned US space program
•
The capsule held 2 men
•
The men were to be gone for 2 weeks
•
The new launch vehicle for Gemini was called Titan II
Capsule size was 150% the size of the Mercury capsule. Unlike the Mercury capsule, 
there is no abort tower on the Gemini capsule.  Gemini astronauts had ejection seats 
they were to use in case of emergency. The ejection seats were never used, which was 
a good thing! 
In the 100% oxygen atmosphere of the capsule, any use of the rocket motors on the 
seats would most certainly have resulted in the deaths of the astronauts. Wally 
Schirra joked “We’d have been a pair of Roman Candles!”  
Astronauts sometimes referred to the capsule as the “Gus Mobile” because Gus 
Grissom was, as were many other astronauts, an engineer and contributed greatly to 
the overall design and function of the Gemini capsule.  
On Gemini 7, a sign saying “Home Sweet Home” was placed in the capsule to 
emphasize that the capsule would be home to the crew for 2 weeks. 
There were 12 total Gemini missions – 2 unmanned (Gemini 1 - 2)and 10 manned 
(Gemini 3-12)
Goals for Gemini Program:
1.
Put 2 men into space for 2 weeks
2.
Rendezvous and dock with orbiting vehicles (fly near and then connect with)
3.
Maneuver vehicles that were docked
4.
Perfect the process of reentry and landing
An early design for the Gemini capsule involved the ability to land on land. This idea 
was abandoned at the time but revived when NASA was developing the reusable STS 
(Space Transport System) also known as the Space Shuttle.

Gemini 3:
•
The first manned Gemini flight
•
There was a piece of unauthorized cargo – a corned beef sandwich from Wolfie’s 
Restaurant was snuck on board. 
•
The sandwich was purchased by (Textbook) Wally Schirra and carried on the 
flight by John Young.  
•
During the mission’s planned food tasting experiment, John Young 
presented Gus Grissom with the sandwich, which was at this point 2 days 
old and very unappetizing. The sandwich practically disintegrated as soon as 
Young removed it from his pocket. 
•
NASA was none too pleased –all those crumbs in their capsule!  It was 
thought that the crumbs could have posed a hazard and so personal cargo 
was more closely scrutinized in flights after that.  
•
There was quite an uproar at NASA and even in Congress. Deke Slayton later 
wrote in his autobiography that he was aware of the sandwich prior to 
launch.
•
Gemini 3 was also the last time NASA allowed the astronauts to name their 
capsules.
•
Gus wanted to name the craft Wapasha after a Native American tribe from 
his home state of Indiana. It was pointed out that this sounded a lot like the 
“Wabash Cannon Ball”, so Gus decided to poke a little fun at the mishap of 
the sinking of the Liberty Bell and called the Gemini 3 capsule Molly Brown. 
This is after a song entitled “The Unsinkable Molly Brown” from a Broadway 
show of the same name about the famous Titanic survivor Margaret 
“Molly” Brown.  
•
NASA did not really see the humor in naming this capsule Molly Brown but 
found it more appealing than his next choice – the Titanic.  
•
Thereafter, the spacecraft were known officially by their numbers, but given 
affectionate nicknames by their crews.

The Soviets also continued their manned space program in an attempt to get to the 
Moon. 
The second manned Soviet program was called Voskhod and flew crews of 2-3.  
Voskhod 1 was a 3-man crew that included the first doctor in space.  
Voskhod 2 was a crew of 2 – Alexi Leonov and Pavel Belyayev.  
•
On this flight, Leonov became the first man to leave the safety of his craft and 
perform an EVA (extra-vehicular activity or spacewalk).  
•
While on his EVA, Leonov’s suit over-inflated and he could not fit back into the 
airlock to return to his capsule.  
•
He slowly bled off some of the pressure until the suit returned to a size that 
allowed him to return to the interior of the capsule. 
•
During re-entry, the pair’s capsule landed far north of their intended landing zone 
in the Ural Mountains in March.  
•
They endured the cold night in the capsule after hearing wolves howling outside.  
•
Crews were able to cut through the pine trees and bring the crew out on skis the 
next day, leaving the capsule to be recovered later.

Gemini 4:
•
Crew: Jim McDivitt and Edward White
•
Ed White performed the first American EVA which lasted about 20 minutes.  
•
Through his experiences, it was discovered that spacewalking was more 
difficult than imagined, but improvements were made to procedures for 
EVAs and space suits for future astronauts. 
•
The golden hose in the display case in front of the trainer was very 
important for EVAs.  This is called a tether and it secured the space walking 
astronaut to the capsule while he was in space and provided him with 
oxygen and communications.  
•
It was also learned during the Gemini 4 mission that after 4 days in space, 
astronauts begin to lose calcium from their bones (bone decalcification).  This is a 
medical issue that doctors and scientists continue to study, as astronauts have a 
very high incidence of kidney stones as a result of this calcium loss.

The Apollo Program
Apollo Overview:
•
The Apollo program was the 3rd American space program. 
•
After the success of the Mercury and Gemini programs, the Americans finally had 
all the tools necessary for a trip to the moon. 
•
The goals of the program were simple – Get a man to the moon and return him 
safely to the Earth. 
•
The first crew of Apollo astronauts – Gus Grissom, Ed White, and Roger Chaffee –
were killed on the launch pad during a test. 
•
There was a fire inside the capsule, which spread rapidly due to the 100% 
oxygen atmosphere. 
•
The tragedy led to a full redesign of the Apollo spacecraft into a much safer 
and more reliable vehicle than the first version. 
•
Although the crew did not fly their mission, NASA still honors them by 
naming them as the crew of Apollo 1. 
•
Apollo missions 2-6 were unmanned test flights of the newly redesigned Apollo 
spacecraft as well as the Saturn V rocket. 
•
Apollo 7 was the first manned Apollo flight.
•
The astronauts launched aboard a Saturn 1-B rocket and successfully tested 
the Apollo spacecraft in low-Earth orbit. 
•
Apollo 8 became the first mission to successfully orbit the moon and return to 
Earth. 
•
Apollo 9 tested the systems of the lunar module in low-Earth orbit
•
Apollo 10 was the lunar landing “dress rehearsal”, in which the lunar module was 
flown down to 10 miles away from the moon, and the abort systems were tested. 
•
Apollo 11 became the first mission to land on the moon on July 20, 1969. 
•
All subsequent Apollo missions (with the exception of 13) were successful moon 
landings. 
•
Apollo 17 was the final mission to the moon.
•
As of right now, no human has set foot upon the lunar surface since Eugene Cernan 
stepped off in 1972.

Apollo crews were made up of a group of 3 astronauts. Of those three, 2 would walk on the moon. 
The third crew member piloted the Apollo spacecraft, orbiting the moon while his two companions 
were walking on the surface. Each astronaut was assigned a specific position in the crew. The three 
positions were the Commander, the Lunar Module Pilot, and the Command Module Pilot. 
The Commander –
The Apollo Commander was the astronaut in charge of the 
mission. Most mission commanders were experienced 
astronauts. During the launch of the Saturn V, which was 
controlled by the computer, the Commander had the power to 
throw the abort switch, activating the launch escape tower. The 
Commander, along with the Lunar Module Pilot, was one of the 
crew members who would walk on the moon. Interestingly it was 
the Commander – not the Lunar Module Pilot – who piloted the 
LM and landed on the moon. 
The Lunar Module Pilot –
“Lunar Module Caretaker” is a more accurate title, as the LMP 
did not actually fly the LM – This was the Commander’s job. The 
LMP was instead responsible for having an intimate 
understanding of the systems of the LM and making sure it was 
operating as it should be. The LMP accompanied the Commander 
down to the surface of the moon. Interestingly, it was common 
for the LMP to have little experience before being assigned to 
walk on the moon. In fact, every LMP after Buzz Aldrin (Alan 
Bean, Fred Haise, Edgar Mitchell, James Irwin, Charlie Duke, 
Harrison Schmitt) was a rookie who had never even been to 
space before! 
The Command Module Pilot –
The Command Module Pilot, or CMP, is often thought of as the 
most boring job. Everybody knows Neil Armstrong and Buzz 
Aldrin, the first moonwalkers, but fewer people recall the name 
of the third crew member of Apollo 11. However, CMPs such as 
Michael Collins have an extremely important job. They are 
responsible for piloting the command module, performing 
important maneuvers such as docking with the LM both on the 
way to the moon and once again as it ascends from the lunar 
surface. While their two companions are walking on the moon, 
the CMP orbits above them, taking pictures of the moon to scout 
out future landing sites. One of the most somber duties of the 
CMP is to return the Apollo spacecraft to Earth alone should 
something go wrong, and his two companions not survive. 
Fortunately, this never happened, but it was for this reason that 
the CMP was often a more experienced astronaut than the LMP.

The Saturn V:
The Saturn V (say “five”) remains to this day the only launch 
vehicle to take men to the Moon, and for that reason is 
sometimes referred to as the Moon Rocket.  
There are only 3 originals left today—the one inside the Davidson 
Center, one at Johnson Space Center in Houston, and one at 
Kennedy Space Center in Florida.  
The Saturn V was a liquid propelled, expendable rocket—i.e. a 
one-use vehicle (no part is reusable-the only part that is 
recovered is the command module, which is never reused).
The Saturn V was proposed by Wernher von Braun in 1957 and 
was designed by the Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC), located 
just south of the Space & Rocket Center on Redstone Arsenal.  
The Saturn V in the Davidson Center was the first full scale Saturn 
V to be completed by MSFC. 
•
Although never flown, it was stacked and used for dynamic 
testing at MSFC.  
•
The testing included structural tests, guidance and flight 
control systems, determining the ability to mate the stages of 
the rocket and how to best transport the vehicle to the 
Kennedy Space Center.  
•
450 hours of testing resulted in minor modifications that were 
incorporated before the first Saturn V launch in November 
1967.
•
After testing, the launch vehicle was assembled at the 
Alabama Space Science Center (later renamed the U.S. Space 
& Rocket Center) to allow public viewing.
The model outside was constructed in 1999 for the 30th 
anniversary of the Apollo 11 moon landing.  
•
The USSRC director at the time was Mike Wing.  
•
It is a scale model, so if the launch vehicle inside were stood 
up, it would look just like the one outside.
The Saturn V is 363 feet tall and weighed more than 6 million 
pounds at lift off.

•
The third stage was built by Douglas Aircraft (later McDonald Douglas) in

At the end of the TLI, the craft will be on an intercept course with the moon.

Launch Escape System:
The tower on top of the capsule is the Launch Escape System
(LES). This system has smaller rockets that can be fired if needed 
to remove the Command Module and astronauts from the rest of 
the vehicle should there be an emergency during launch.  This 
removes the astronauts from the source of the fuel or propellant. 
In the absence of an emergency, the LES was routinely jettisoned 
about 20 or 30 seconds after the launch vehicle's second-stage 
ignition (this is about 3 ½ - 4 minutes after launch at an altitude 
of roughly 295,000 feet [just under 55 miles])
The Apollo Spacecraft:
On top of the Saturn V sits the Apollo spacecraft. It is important to note that the 
Saturn V is technically the name given to the booster which carries the Apollo 
spacecraft into orbit and beyond. 
After the astronauts separate from the 3rd stage of the Saturn V and undock the LM, 
the Saturn V has served its purpose and it is the Apollo spacecraft which then carries 
the astronauts the rest of the way to the moon and then back home when the mission 
is complete. 
The Apollo spacecraft is composed of 2 distinct parts –
• the Command Module, in which the three astronauts sit 
• the Service module, which contains oxygen, water, and provides electrical power to 
the systems of the Command Module. 
When these two components are connected, they are referred to as the Command-
Service Module (CSM). 
The final step for the astronauts before reentry into the atmosphere is to jettison the 
Service Module. Only the Command Module will reenter the atmosphere at the end of 
the mission, safely carrying the astronauts to splashdown.

Lunar Module:
The LM is a 2-stage vehicle –
•
the upper ascent stage in silver
•
the lower descent stage in gold 
The shiny, foil looking covering is many layers of a special wrapping, primarily aluminized mylar, 
designed to protect the craft from intense heat and cold, and damage from micro-meteorites and 
other space debris. Mylar is a brand name for a type of stretched polyester film. It is often coated with 
extremely thin layers of metal materials, as it was for the LM (covered in aluminum).
Grumman designed the lander to land on the surface of the moon, and then lift off from the lunar 
surface and rendezvous with the command module in lunar orbit. The LM would be the home of the 
two astronauts during their stay on the lunar surface, providing them with oxygen and life support.  
When lunar orbit was achieved, and the landing site within range, the Mission Commander and the LM 
Pilot would take their positions in the LM and separate from the command module.  The LM would 
then execute a computer-controlled series of main and auxiliary engine burns in conjunction with the 
landing radar to reach essentially a hover state above the landing site. At this point, the crew would 
take control of the LM.  When the LM was about five feet above the surface of the moon, sensors 
would contact the moon and indicate to the LM pilot to cut the engine, causing the entire craft to 
settle down on the surface.
The LM was initially designed as a rather heavy craft with large observation windows and chairs for the 
astronauts to sit in. However, this design was going to be too heavy. Weight was at a premium for the 
NASA engineers designing the Apollo Spacecraft and the Saturn V, so the chairs and windows were 
removed. When landing on the moon, the astronauts would stand upright and use a small observation 
window to view the surface of the moon below. Additional weight was shed by making the lower stage 
of the LM a metal skeleton covered in mylar and foil.

Ablative Shield:
All spacecraft designed by the United States prior to the space shuttle utilized layered 
ablative materials for heat protection during re-entry into the Earth’s atmosphere. 
Ablative means that as the material is subjected to the heat of re-entry, the material 
would absorb a certain amount of heat, turn to a liquid, then “burn off” the craft, taking 
the heat away from the capsule and keeping it cool near the top, where the astronauts 
sat. The heat of re-entry is quite intense, up to 6000 degrees F, and without some kind of 
heat protection, re-entry would be impossible to survive.  
The thermal protection system is a honeycomb matrix that is bonded to a stainless-steel 
shell.  The ablative material (Avco brand of an epoxy resin) was then inserted into the 
honeycomb cells with a device that looked much like a caulking gun. The thickness of the 
material before use varied from .7 to 2.7 inches. Each cell was X-ray inspected, and if the 
slightest crack or imperfection was found in a particular cell, it was completely drilled out 
before being refilled and reinspected. 
The parachute is an original and was used during re-entry to slow the capsule down 
before splash down into the Pacific.  They are so large that they are folded about in half 
to be on display here. Three were used on the capsules.

Apollo 13: 
•
Crew: Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert, Fred Haise
•
Fuel cells take oxygen and hydrogen and combine them to create power and water.  
This was the main source of electricity for the Gemini, Apollo and even Shuttle craft.  
•
The Apollo 13 crew experienced an O2 tank explosion almost 56 hours into the mission. 
•
The loss of this tank caused a chain reaction of shorts and failures.  This limited power, 
heat, water and clean air and led to a shutdown of the CM and the 3 astronauts moved

•
The LiOH (Lithium Hydroxide) filters for air cleaning were taxed, and with guidance 
from mission control the crew built an adapter for the CM filter to accept the LM hoses. 
(The filters for the CM were square and the filters for the LM were round!)  
•
One of the many other issues was that the LM was created to support a crew of 2 for 1-
2 days, not a crew of 3 for 4 days.  
•
The LM also had to power down to minimal usage to ensure power long enough to 
return the crew home. 
•
Because the crew was returned home safely but the mission objective was to land on 
the Moon, Jim Lovell referred to Apollo 13 as a successful failure.  
•
Flight Director Gene Kranz would call this NASA’s finest hour.  (O2 tank explodes approx.

•

•

Mobile Quarantine Facility:
In the 1960s when we went to the moon, NASA was not sure what was “out” there 
and if astronauts and the samples were infected with moon germs or viruses that 
would spread worldwide causing disease or infections.  
The LRL (Lunar Receiving Laboratory) was a larger quarantine facility built at Johnson 
Space Center in Houston, TX, but they needed a way to isolate the astronauts and 
lunar samples during transport from splashdown to Houston.  
The MQF was used to transport the astronauts from the recovery ship to the receiving 
lab. The travel trailer could accommodate up to six people for as long as ten days, as 
they were transported to the LRL.   
Once in the raft the astronauts were scrubbed down and their flight suits were thrown 
back into the CM module that was floating in the water. They were then put into a 
zippered nylon head-to-toe coverall which was equipped with a respirator called a 
Biological Isolation Garment (or BIG suit).  Astronauts were airlifted from the CM to 
the recovery ship where they entered the MQF.  The BIG suits were removed, and the 
astronauts were able to wear NASA flight suits for the rest of the quarantine period.  
While the MQF doesn’t look very spacious, compared to the capsule this was a lot of 
room.  The trailer contained a kitchen, living and sleeping quarters, and a commode.  
The MQF and the astronauts were flown to the Lunar Receiving Laboratory, Ellington 
Air Force Base in Houston Texas.  Astronauts entered the Lunar Receiving Laboratory 
and finished their quarantine time there. Total time in the MQF was about 65 hours.  
Astronauts were joined in MQF by a doctor and an assistant for cooking and cleaning.
The units were used for Apollo 11, 12, and 14. NASA planned to use them for the first 
4 missions, but there was no need to utilize the MQF for Apollo 13. The crew of 13 did 
not actually make it to the moon, so there was no need to quarantine them after their 
mission. 
MQF001 was last seen in MS, possibly destroyed, and thought to be slated for Apollo 
13.  
MQF002 was used for Apollo 12- and is the one here in the Davidson Center.
MQF003 was used for Apollo 11- and located at the Hazy Center, Chantilly VA.
MQF004 was used for Apollo 14- and located at USS Hornet Museum- in Alameda, CA.
No germs were found so the quarantine process ended after the Apollo 14 flight.

Skylab:
Apollo 17 was the last lunar mission. No human has set foot upon the moon since Gene 
Cernan stepped off the surface in 1972. 
NASA may have been done with lunar landings, but they had been planning their next 
project since the early 60s – a space station. 
Launched in 1973, Skylab was America’s first space station.  It was created by re-
purposing the third stage of an unused Saturn V launch vehicle.  
The space station, unmanned at the time of launch, suffered damage. The meteorite 
shield which also helped with thermal control was ripped away, and in the process the 
solar panels – the main source of electrical power – were damaged as well. 
There were 3 manned missions to Skylab.  The first manned mission had to initially 
concentrate on repairs to the orbiting laboratory.  
The station never really operated at 100% due to the damage that it had suffered. In

The Shuttle Program
The Space Transportation System, or STS, received its funding in April 1972.  John Young 
was walking on the moon during the Apollo 16 mission when the announcement came 
that the Shuttle program was a go.  
On April 12, 1981, the first shuttle Columbia flew its maiden voyage with astronauts 
John Young and Robert Crippen on board.  Columbia’s first launch was potentially the 
most dangerous launch ever attempted by American astronauts.  Before the Space 
Shuttle, every piece of hardware ever launched was completely tested before humans 
were allowed to enter the vehicle. However, to fly the Space Shuttle, somebody must 
sit in the cockpit of the Orbiter to land it. This means that uncrewed test flights of the 
spacecraft would be impossible. The maiden launch of Columbia was the first time all 
the Space Shuttle engines were ignited at the same time, and all systems worked 
perfectly. 
The Space Shuttle Main Engine (SSME) was the most powerful reusable fueled engine 
in the world at the time.  It was designed to operate for approximately 8 hours and 
endure up to 55 starts. The 3 main engines of the shuttle orbiter were only used during 
launch. After the shuttle reached orbit, the engines were shut down and were not used 
again until the next launch. The average burn time for the SSMEs was 8 minutes per 
flight, which would mean that the main engines would only have to be replaced 3 times 
over the expected life of each orbiter.  These engines were throttle-controlled and 
needed to be throttled back during liftoff to keep the orbiter from shaking apart when 
it passed through Max-Q, or maximum aerodynamic pressure.  At full power, the SSMEs 
produced as much power as 13 Hoover Dams.
Even though the 3 main engines were powerful, they were not enough to place the 
shuttle into Earth’s orbit.  It needed 2 Solid Rocket Boosters (SRBs) to give the shuttle 
more thrust for launch.  The SRBs had an equivalent power of 14,700 six-axle 
locomotives and were re-usable for up to 15 flights.  The SRBs were jettisoned after the 
shuttle traveled approximately 30 miles down range, leaving the three main engines to 
complete the next 130 miles to Earth orbit.  The SRBs were retrieved from the ocean, 
inspected, and repaired.  If deemed fit, they would be refueled for another mission.  
Each SRB weighed just over a million pounds when full of fuel.

The large rust colored tank is the External Tank, or ET.  The ET is not really covered in 
rust, it is an aluminum tank covered with foam.  The ET contains the liquid hydrogen and 
liquid oxygen that the three SSMEs use for fuel.  The ET provides 64,868 pounds of fuel 
per minute to the SSMEs.  When the tank was empty, it was released from the orbiter 
and burned up in the upper atmosphere.  The ET is the only part of the shuttle that 
could not be re-used.
The Space Shuttle can send 22 tons of cargo into space such as experiments, satellites 
and space station components. About 80% of the components for the International 
Space Station were carried into orbit by the Space Shuttle.  The payload bay of the 
shuttle has enough room to carry an average sized school bus into space.
The orbiter travels about 17,500 miles per hour in space, and it needed to be heat 
resistant in order to withstand re-entry through Earth’s atmosphere. It was covered with 
approximately 24,300 tiles and 2,300 Flexible Insulation Blankets which are made of a 
compound found in sand.  These tiles are very light weight and each one has a unique 
design, depending upon its placement on the orbiter.  These tiles are designed to 
absorb the heat of re-entry, and then radiate that captured heat away from the orbiter.  
The color markings on the orbiter designate different colored tiles.  The white colored 
tiles are used in areas where the heat of re-entry is not as strong, and the black tiles are 
used in areas where the shuttle receives more heat.  Some tiles do get damaged and 
may fall off, however, they can be replaced when the orbiter is being prepared for its 
next flight.
When the orbiter lands it is much like a glider.  For every foot of forward motion, the 
orbiter falls five feet.  Shuttle astronauts train in special aircraft to learn how to land the 
orbiter perfectly each time.

NASA’s Space Launch System
The SLS is the most powerful rocket NASA has ever built - it has almost 1 million more 
pounds of thrust than the Saturn V. 
Many of the components are recycled from the Space Shuttle, such as the RS-25 
engines on the bottom of the core stage, and the 2 solid rocket boosters which flank 
the core stage. These pieces are assembled in a configuration more like the Saturn V 
– a multistage, non-reusable rocket. 
The SLS will enable astronauts to travel back to the moon for the first time since 
1972, including the first female astronaut to walk on the moon. NASA also has its 
sights set on deep space as well, planning on using the SLS to put the first astronauts 
on the surface of Mars. 
The Artemis Project
With Artemis missions, NASA will land the first woman and first person of color on 
the Moon, as well as explore more of the lunar surface than ever before. NASA will 
collaborate with commercial and international partners to establish the first long-
term presence on the Moon. The goal is to use what we learn on and around the 
Moon to take the next giant leap: sending the first astronauts to Mars.
Artemis 1 –
•
Artemis 1 was the first flight of the SLS and the Orion spacecraft. 
•
It launched on November 16th, 2022, with an uncrewed spacecraft.
•
It used the Block 1 configuration, which stands at 322 feet tall - taller than the 
Statue of Liberty - and weighs 5.75 million lbs. 
•
During launch and ascent, SLS produced 8.8 million lbs. of thrust, 15% more 
thrust than the Saturn V rocket.
•
After the rocket burned through the fuel in the boosters and core stage, it 
dropped them. SLS does not carry extra fuel or propulsion systems necessary to 
return any stages to Earth for reuse because. The solid rocket boosters separate 
two minutes into the flight, and the core stage falls away around eight minutes 
after launch.

Building the Rocket:
The Boeing Company in Huntsville, Alabama, builds the SLS core stages, including the 
avionics that controls the vehicle during flight. 
Towering more than 212 feet, the core stage holds 730,000 gallons of liquid hydrogen 
and liquid oxygen that fuel the RS-25 engines. 
The SLS avionics computer software is developed at NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center 
in Huntsville.
