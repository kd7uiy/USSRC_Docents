This exhibit is a “Mock POIC”, and POIC (pronounced like “pock”) stands for Payload Operations 
Integration Center. This area is designed to look exactly like the real POIC, which is located here 
in Huntsville at Marshall Space Flight Center.
There are lots of screens on the wall above. Most are pre-recorded footage, but 3 of the screens 
are live (circled). The top left screen of the map, the center screen with the time, and the far-
right screen.
•
The top left screen is world map that shows where the ISS is currently located, it shows its 
previous and future path, as well as its altitude and speed. 
•
The ISS orbits the Earth at an average speed of 17,500 mph (28,000kph)
•
The average altitude of the ISS is about 250 miles (400 km) above the Earth’s surface.
•
At this speed, it only takes 90 minutes to orbit the Earth 1 time – 45 minutes of daytime, 
and 45 minutes of nighttime. The ISS orbits the Earth 16 times a day, so the crew will 
witness 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every 24 hours. 
•
The clock in the center shows GMT (Greenwich Mean Time), which is the time zone used by 
both the crew on the ISS as well as the crew working on the ISS from the ground. The clock 
also shows the current time here in Huntsville – Central Standard Time.
•
The far-right screen sometimes has a live view of the Earth as the ISS flies over, sometimes
there’s an EVA on the camera, and sometimes it’s just dark.
End by directing the guests’ attention to the screen you will be presenting from.
Begin by greeting the guests and welcoming them to Science on Orbit!

Slide 1 – Paperwork Signed
On January 29, 1998, representatives from 15 different countries 
met in Washington, DC to lay the framework for the building of an 
International Space Station, which would serve as a long-term 
science and research facility in low-Earth orbit that the entire 
world could benefit from. 
These 15 nations are represented by the flags on the roof, and are 
as follows: 
1. The United States 
2. Russia 
3. Canada 
4. Japan 
+ 11 nations from the European Space Agency, 
or ESA – Belgium, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, The 
Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, and the 
United Kingdom 
The first piece of the ISS would launch before 1998 was over.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="5" img="pages/page-005.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 2 – Zarya Module 
This is the first piece of the ISS, a module called Zarya. Designed
and built by the Russians, Zarya was launched on November 20, 
1998. 
“Zarya” translates to “new dawn” or “sunrise” in English.
On the ISS, components are classified based on their 
configuration as either “modules” or “nodes”
•
Module: 
•
like a large room in a house
•
Where astronauts will either live or do work
•
Node:
•
like a hallway in a house
•
connects modules
•
usually built with docking ports for the purpose of 
attaching new modules to the ISS
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="6" img="pages/page-006.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 3 – Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan
Zarya was launched from the Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan, where Russia 
currently launches all their crew from. 
•
Russia does this because Kazakhstan was once part of the Soviet Union. 
•
The USSR doesn’t exist anymore, although the Baikonur Cosmodrome is 
technically Russian-owned property in Kazakhstan.
From 2011 – 2020, this was the only place in the world where astronauts would 
launch from to join the ISS.
•
When America’s Space Shuttle program ended in 2011, the USA did not have a 
vehicle capable of transporting astronauts to low-earth orbit.
•
NASA paid Russia to send American astronauts to the ISS in a Soyuz rocket, which
cost about $80 million per astronaut.
In 2020, the SpaceX Crew Dragon capsule launched the first Americans into space 
since the Shuttle Program ended in 2011.
Private companies such as SpaceX and Boeing offer a cheaper cost at $50 million per 
seat, so private companies will take over duties of shuttling American astronauts to 
low-earth orbit. 
This will allow NASA the freedom to set their sights on the more distant reaches of 
space.
{{< /slide >}}

The second piece to join Zarya on the ISS was Unity, a node 
built by America. 
It was designed and built here in Huntsville, Alabama at the 
Marshall Space Flight Center.

Slide 7 – Video of Zarya and Unity docking using the Canada Arm
VIDEO –
Unity and Zarya were docked together in low-Earth orbit on 
December 6, 1998. 
Docking in space is an incredibly difficult procedure. Remember 
that both pieces of the station, as well as the Shuttle Endeavour, 
are orbiting the Earth at 17,500 miles per hour. Every movement 
must be precise and carefully executed. 
The final docking was achieved with the help of the Canadarm, a 
robotic arm on the Space Shuttle. Most new pieces of the 
ISS were put together in this way. 
When Unity and Zarya were docked, a truly International Space 
Station was created.

{{< slide label="Page" n="11" img="pages/page-011.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 8 – ISS size and weight
Here is what the ISS looks like today, in its complete configuration. 
It is about the same size as an American football field, spanning
from end zone to end zone. 
If it were to be brought down to Earth, it would weigh 
approximately 1 million pounds. 
In the microgravity of space, which is 1/1 millionth of Earth’s 
gravity, it weighs only about 1 pound. This means that if you could 
ground yourself in space, it would be easy to push the ISS around 
with a single finger!
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="12" img="pages/page-012.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 9 – Video timelapse of pieces being added to the ISS
VIDEO –
This time lapse shows each new piece of the ISS as it was added.
Every piece of the station was designed to be modular, meaning it can 
be placed anywhere on the station. 
As the ISS was being built, it went through several different 
configurations. For example, the video will show the solar panels 
originally on the top of the station and then being moved to the sides 
as construction went on.

{{< slide label="Page" n="13" img="pages/page-013.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 10 – View of crews coming together
VIDEO –
Crews are shuttled to the ISS by the Russian Soyuz rocket. Now we have other 
capsules traveling to the ISS as well, like the SpaceX Crew Dragon capsule, and 
other companies like Boeing are developing their own vehicles, such as the 
Boeing Starliner. 
Most of the time, astronauts will launch in groups of 3, with typically 6 crew 
members in total on station. Each group of 3 stays up in space for an average 
of 6 months, during which they will participate in 2 missions. 
•
As new crew members arrive on station to begin their first mission, there 
will be astronauts on station who are finished with their second mission 
and ready to come home. 
•
The new crew members replace the old in this unique crew rotation. 
Some notable astronauts who performed more than two missions in one trip 
are American astronaut Scott Kelley and Space Camp Alumna Christina Koch, 
who both spent nearly 1 year on the station studying the long-term effects of 
spaceflight on the human body.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="14" img="pages/page-014.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 11 – How to See the ISS in the sky
Anybody who wishes to catch a glimpse of the ISS may do so without 
ever going into space. It is easily visible from the Earth, as it is the 
third brightest object in the sky, after the sun and the moon. 
Clear nights and early mornings offer the best opportunities to spot 
the ISS. It looks like an airplane in the sky, except it moves much 
faster and doesn’t blink. 
There are numerous resources available to see when the space 
station will be visible from a particular location.
www.spotthestation.nasa.gov is the official NASA website dedicated 
to displaying the best viewing periods to catch a glimpse of the ISS. 
There are also phone apps, such as ISS Tracker and ISS Spotter, which 
perform the same function.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="15" img="pages/page-015.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 12 – Video of inside the ISS + Samantha Cristoforetti
VIDEO –
A view of the inside of the station. 
The woman in the video with the short, spiky hair is named Samantha 
Cristoforetti. She is Italian, but she came to Space Camp when she was in 
the United States as an exchange student. 
While she was here, she fell in love with space and later became a captain in 
the Italian Air Force and an astronaut with the European Space Agency. 
She was on station for over 200 days, from November 2014 to June 2015, 
setting the record at the time for the longest female spaceflight. 
She also was responsible for an espresso machine being placed on the 
station. It was dubbed the “ISSpresso”, and cost $1.9 million to launch into 
space.

Each new crew gets to create its own new mission patch, giving 
input to the design, colors, and symbols.

{{< slide label="Page" n="17" img="pages/page-017.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 14 – Robonaut
Being an astronaut is a dangerous job. Humans are not capable of surviving the 
harsh vacuum of space without expensive and bulky suits which require several 
hours to put on and take off. 
NASA would rather keep its astronauts inside the relative safety of the station, 
where they can devote more of their time to performing science experiments. The 
goal is for the astronauts to be scientists first, and astronauts second. 
For this reason, many efforts have been made to create robotic assistants who 
would be capable of performing some of the more dangerous or mundane tasks. 
One example is our friend Robonaut here. The idea is that he will be able to go 
outside instead of an astronaut and perform some of the more complex 
maintenance operations on the outside of the station. 
He does not have to worry about the lack of oxygen or the extreme temperatures, 
and if he happens to drift off into space, he is replaceable – an astronaut is NOT! 
Robonaut has spent stretches of time on station but is currently back on Earth 
receiving some upgrades in order to better assist his astronaut crewmates.
{{< /slide >}}

Slide 15 – Where is Marshall Space Flight Center
A few miles down the road from the US Space and Rocket Center is 
the Redstone Arsenal, which is home to the Marshall Space Flight 
Center. 
Located on MSFC is the Payload Operations Integration Center 
(POIC – pronounced “Pock”).
The Science on Orbit presentation space is designed to be a mock-
up of the real POIC over at MSFC.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="19" img="pages/page-019.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 16 – POIC and its function
The people at the POIC are responsible for overseeing all the 
science experiments which are being done onboard the station.
Science experiments on the ISS are referred to as “Payloads”, hence 
the name. 
Remember that the ISS is basically just a big science lab floating 
around in low-Earth orbit, so there may be as many as 200 
experiments going on at one time, both short and long-term. 
Every single one of these experiments are overseen by the folks at 
the POIC right here in Huntsville, Alabama.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="20" img="pages/page-020.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 17 – 3 major space centers in the USA
Marshall Space Flight Center is one of three major space centers 
here in the southern United States. 
•
Johnson Space center in Houston, Texas serves as main mission 
control for the ISS and is responsible for the training of all the 
astronauts. 
•
In addition to overseeing the numerous science experiments on 
the station, MSFC is also capable of functioning as a backup to 
mission control if necessary. 
•
Finally, the Kennedy Space Center in Cape Canaveral, Florida 
handles the launching of resupply missions. Since 2020, Kennedy 
has also been the launching point for astronauts traveling to the 
ISS with SpaceX
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="21" img="pages/page-021.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 18 – Science experiments on ISS
One of the greatest assets of the ISS is its accessibility. 
Scientists here on Earth who want to perform experiments in zero 
gravity, but do not want to go through the extensive astronaut 
training may send up their experiments to the ISS, where a trained 
astronaut scientist will carry it out for them. 
The experiment will be monitored from the Earth and the data is  
then sent back to the original scientist. 
In this way, more and more people are able to carry out unique 
science experiments which could not be performed here on the 
Earth.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="22" img="pages/page-022.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 19 – Video of racks
VIDEO –
Most modules on the ISS are lined with what are called racks. Around 
the same size and weight as a refrigerator, these racks are used to 
store experiments. Since there are so many experiments going on 
aboard the station, lots of storage space is a must. 
There are racks for every imaginable type of experiment, from cold 
storage to storage for living animals, plants, even racks for storing 
food and water, used in living modules. 
Back here on Earth, the men and women who oversee the 
experiments on the station are known as Payload Rack Officers, or 
PROs. It is their job to ensure that each experiment is being carried 
out safely and appropriately, as well as to monitor the data coming 
from each experiment.
{{< /slide >}}

Slide 20 – Video of POIC employees
VIDEO –
The POIC is staffed around the clock every single day of the year.
The staff Is constantly monitoring every experiment making sure 
that everything is being carried out effectively.

Slide 21 – Video of POD
VIDEO –
The POIC is led by the Payload Operations Director, or POD. 
They manage the day-to-day operations of experiments 
onboard the station, and report directly to mission control in 
Houston.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="25" img="pages/page-025.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Slide 22 – Sally Ride Earth Kam
The ISS also engages in numerous education and outreach programs. 
This is the Sally Ride EarthKAM. It is sponsored by the US Space and 
Rocket Center. 
Students and teachers around the world may participate in this 
program and request a picture be taken of any spot on the Earth 
that they want. 
It is sent to us here at the USSRC, and then distributed around the 
world to whoever requested it. 
Many teachers request pictures of their schools or their towns for 
lessons. It has proven a useful tool for scientific study of all kinds. 
For example, some teachers repeatedly request pictures of the same 
glaciers to instruct their students about climate change.
{{< /slide >}}
