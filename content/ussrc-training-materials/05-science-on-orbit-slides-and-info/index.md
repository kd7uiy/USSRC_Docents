Welcome To
Welcome the guests to the Science on Orbit presentation. Begin each presentation by 
talking the audience through the television screens that are behind you on the wall. Almost 
all of them show prerecorded footage from previous missions, but draw special attention 
to the screens which are live: the world map, and the clock. Explain that the map shows 
the position of the ISS now, its orbital paths, as well as its current altitude and speed. The 
ISS orbits the Earth at an average speed of 17,500mph (28,000kph) and at an average 
altitude of 250 miles (400km) above the Earth’s surface. At this speed it takes only 90 
minutes for it to complete 1 Orbit, 45 minutes of which will be daytime, and 45 which will 
be nighttime. The ISS orbits the Earth 16 times a day, so the crew will witness 16 sunrises 
and sunsets every 24 hours. The clock shows GMT (Greenwich Mean Time), which is used 
as a standard time both for the crew of the ISS and everybody who works on it from the 
ground. The clock also shows CST (Central Standard Time), which we operate on here in 
Huntsville, AL. After explaining these basic facts, begin the presentation.

{{< slide label="Page" n="2" img="pages/page-002.webp" collapse="true" >}}
On January 29, 1998, representatives from 15 different countries met in Washington, DC to 
lay the framework for the building of an International Space Station, which would serve as 
a long term science and research facility in low-Earth orbit that the entire world could 
benefit from. These 15 nations are (They are represented by the flags on the roof) 1. The 
United States 2. Russia 3. Canada 4. Japan 5. 11 nations from the European Space Agency, 
or ESA (in alphabetical order on the ceiling – Belgium. Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, The 
Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom) The first piece 
of the ISS would launch before 1998 was over.
2
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="3" img="pages/page-003.webp" collapse="true" >}}
This is the first piece of the ISS. The module “Zarya”. Designed and built by the Russians, 
Zarya was launched on November 20, 1998. Zarya translates to “new dawn” or “sunrise” in 
English. On the ISS, components are classified based on their configurations as either 
“Modules” or “Nodes”. Here is a good analogy – A module on a space station is like a large 
room in a house, where astronauts will either live or do work. A node is similar to a hallway 
between rooms – they mostly serve as pathways to different modules. They are usually 
built with several docking ports for the purpose of attaching new modules to the ISS.
3
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="4" img="pages/page-004.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Zarya was launched on November 20, 1998, from the Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan. 
Russia performs all of its launches from Kazakhstan. Why? Because at one point, 
Kazakhstan was part of the Soviet Union. Although the USSR does not exist anymore, the 
Baikonur Cosmodrome is technically Russian-owned property in Kazakhstan. From 2011-
2020 This was the only spot in the world that astronauts could launch from to get to the 
ISS. Why? Between the End of the Space Shuttle program in 2011 and the success of the 
SpaceX Crew Dragon capsule in late 2020, the United States did not have a vehicle capable 
of sending humans into low-Earth orbit. NASA actually paid the Russians to send up our 
astronauts for us in the Soyuz rocket, at a cost of around $80 million per seat. Private 
companies such as SpaceX and Boeing offer a cheaper option at around $50 million per 
seat. Going forward, private companies will take over the duties of shuttling astronauts to 
low-Earth orbit, allowing space agencies such as NASA the freedom to set their sights on 
the more distant reaches of space.

VIDEO – Unity and Zarya were docked together in low-Earth orbit on December 6, 1998. 
Docking in space is an incredibly difficult procedure. Remember that both pieces of the 
station, as well as the Shuttle Endeavour are orbiting the Earth at 17,500 miles per hour. 
Every movement must be precise and carefully executed. The final docking was achieved 
with the help of the Canadarm, a robotic arm on the Space Shuttle. Most new pieces of the 
ISS were put together in this way. When Unity and Zarya were docked, a truly International 
Space Station was created.

{{< slide label="Page" n="9" img="pages/page-009.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Here is what the ISS looks like today, in its complete configuration. It is about the same size 
as an American football field, including both of the end zones. If it were to be brought 
down to Earth, it would weigh approximately 1 million pounds. In the microgravity of 
space, which is 1/1 millionth of Earth’s gravity, it actually weighs only about 1 pound. This 
means that if you could ground yourself in space, it would be easy to push the ISS around 
with a single finger!

VIDEO – This time lapse shows each new piece of the ISS as it was added. Every piece of 
the station was designed to be modular, meaning it can be placed anywhere on the station. 
As the ISS was being built, it went through several different configurations. For example, 
the video will show the solar panels originally on the top of the station and then being 
moved to the sides as construction went on.

{{< slide label="Page" n="11" img="pages/page-011.webp" collapse="true" >}}
VIDEO – Crews are shuttled to the ISS by the Russian Soyuz rocket or the SpaceX Crew 
Dragon capsule, with other companies like Boeing developing their own vehicles, such as 
the Boeing Starliner. Most of the time, astronauts will launch in groups of 3, with 6 crew 
usually on station. Each group of three stays up in space for an average of 6 months, during 
which they will participate in two missions. As new crew members arrive on station to 
begin their first mission, there will be astronauts on station who are finished with their 
second mission and ready to come home. The new crew members replace the old in this 
unique crew rotation. Some notable astronauts who performed more than two missions in 
one trip are American astronaut Scott Kelley and Space Camp Alumna Christina Koch, who 
both spent nearly 1 year on the station studying the long-term effects of spaceflight on the 
human body.

{{< slide label="Page" n="12" img="pages/page-012.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Anybody who wishes to catch a glimpse of the ISS may do so without ever going into space. 
It is easily visible from the Earth, as it is the third brightest object in the sky, after the sun 
and the moon. Clear nights and early mornings offer the best opportunities to spot the ISS. 
It looks similar to an airplane, except it moves much faster and it never blinks. There are 
numerous resources available to see when the space station will be visible from a 
particular location. www.spotthestation.nasa.gov is the official NASA website dedicated to 
displaying the best viewing periods to catch a glimpse of the ISS. There are also phone 
apps, such as ISS Tracker and ISS Spotter, which perform the same function.

VIDEO – A view of the inside of the station. The woman in the video with the short, spiky 
hair is named Samantha Cristoforetti. She is Italian, but came to Space Camp when she was 
in the United States as an exchange student. While she was here, she fell in love with space 
and later became a captain in the Italian Air Force and an astronaut with the European 
Space Agency. She was on station for over 200 days, from November 2014 to June 2015, 
setting the record at the time for the longest female spaceflight. She also was responsible 
for an espresso machine being placed on the station. It was dubbed the “ISSpresso”, and 
cost $1.9 million to launch into space.

{{< slide label="Page" n="15" img="pages/page-015.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Being an astronaut is a dangerous job. Humans are not capable of surviving the harsh 
vacuum of space without expensive and bulky suits which require several hours to put on 
and take off. NASA would rather keep its astronauts inside the relative safety of the station, 
where they can devote more of their time to performing science experiments. The goal is 
for the astronauts to be scientists first, and astronauts second. For this reason, many 
efforts have been made to create robotic assistants who would be capable of performing 
some of the more dangerous or mundane tasks. One example is our friend Robonaut here. 
The idea is that he will be able to go outside instead of an astronaut and perform some of 
the more complex maintenance operations on the outside of the station. He does not have 
to worry about the lack of oxygen or the extreme temperatures, and if he happens to drift 
off into space, he is replaceable – an astronaut is NOT! Robonaut has spent stretches of 
time on station, but is currently back on Earth receiving some upgrades in order to better 
assist his astronaut crewmates.

{{< slide label="Page" n="16" img="pages/page-016.webp" collapse="true" >}}
A few miles down the road from the US Space and Rocket Center is the Redstone Arsenal, 
which is home to the Marshall Space Flight Center. Located on MSFC is the Payload 
Operations Integration Center (POIC – pronounced “Pock”) The Science on Orbit 
presentation space is designed to be a mock-up of the real POIC over at MSFC. 
16
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="17" img="pages/page-017.webp" collapse="true" >}}
The people at the POIC are responsible for overseeing all of the science experiments which 
are being done onboard the station. Science experiments on the ISS are referred to as 
“Payloads”, hence the name. Remember that the ISS is basically just a big science lab 
floating around in low-Earth orbit, so there may be as many as 200 experiments going on at 
one time, both short and long-term. Each and every one of these experiments are overseen 
by the folks at the POIC right here in Huntsville, Alabama. 
17
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="18" img="pages/page-018.webp" collapse="true" >}}
Marshall Space Flight Center is actually one of three major space centers here in the 
southern United States. Johnson Space center in Houston, Texas serves as main mission 
control for the ISS and also is responsible for the training of all of the astronauts. In 
addition to overseeing the numerous science experiments on the station, MSFC is also 
capable of functioning as a backup to mission control if necessary. Finally, the Kennedy 
Space Center in Cape Canaveral, Florida handles the launching of resupply missions, and 
since 2020, has also been the launching point for astronauts traveling to the ISS with 
SpaceX. 
18
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="19" img="pages/page-019.webp" collapse="true" >}}
One of the greatest assets of the ISS is its accessibility. Scientists here on Earth who want to 
perform experiments in zero gravity, but do not want to go through the extensive astronaut 
training may send up their experiments to the ISS, where a trained astronaut scientist will 
carry it out for them. The experiment will be monitored from the Earth and the data then 
sent back to the original scientist. In this way, more and more people are able to carry out 
unique science experiments which could not be performed here on the Earth. 
19
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide label="Page" n="20" img="pages/page-020.webp" collapse="true" >}}
VIDEO - Most modules on the ISS are lined with what are called racks. Around the same 
size and weight as a refrigerator, these racks are used to store experiments. Since there are 
so many experiments going on aboard the station, lots of storage space is a must. There are 
racks for every imaginable type of experiment, from cold storage to storage for living 
animals, plants, even racks for storing food and water, used in living modules. Back here on 
Earth, the men and women who oversee the experiments on the station are known as 
Payload Rack Officers, or PROs. It is their job to ensure that each experiment is being 
carried out safely and appropriately, as well as to monitor the data coming from each 
experiment.

{{< slide label="Page" n="23" img="pages/page-023.webp" collapse="true" >}}
The ISS also engages in numerous education and outreach programs. This is the Sally Ride 
EarthKAM. It is sponsored by the US Space and Rocket Center. Students and teachers 
around the world may participate in this program and request a picture be taken of any 
spot on the Earth that they want. It is sent to us here at the USSRC, and then distributed 
around the world to whoever requested it. Many teachers request pictures of their schools 
or their towns for lessons. It has proven a useful tool for scientific study of all kinds. For 
example, some teachers repeatedly request pictures of the same glaciers to instruct their 
students about climate change.
