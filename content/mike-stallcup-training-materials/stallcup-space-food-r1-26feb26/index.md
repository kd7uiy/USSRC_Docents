First of all I should clarify that spacecraft (and the astronauts inside them) orbiting the Earth experience what we refer to as 'microgravity,' not zero gravity.
In the early 1960s both the US and the Soviets were developing spacecraft and dealing with the same issues.
When we first sent people into space, neither the US nor the Soviets knew if humans could eat in space.
Would they be able to swallow? Could they drink anything? Would the food be transported properly by the digestive tract? Would the food even be digested in microgravity?
At that time we weren't really interested in how the food would taste in space. We just wanted to know if it would keep the astronauts alive.
Since there is no gravity in orbit you can't put your food on a plate, because it will float away.
Liquids won't stay in a cup. Well, actually they WILL but only under very specific circumstances which I'll talk about later.
Any food that gets loose, such as crumbs, will float around possibly getting into electronics or into the eyes or lungs of astronauts, all of which would be a very bad thing.
  
————-Extra info —————
Explanation of microgravity, sometimes called free-fall.
The pull of gravity at the altitude of ISS (250 miles) is only about 10% less than the pull of gravity on the surface of the Earth.
The 17,500 mph sideways speed (the orbital velocity of ISS) is what creates free-fall.
The astronauts and the vehicle they are in (ISS) are always falling toward the Earth but always missing it. Because the astronauts are going the same speed as their vehicle it appears that they are floating inside the ISS.
If somebody asks how much gravity drops off with distance.
Newton's law of universal gravitation essentially says that every particle attracts every other particle.
This attraction is a function of their relative masses and the distance between them.
The attraction drops off proportionally to the square of their distance.
[Technically, it's inversely proportional the the square of their distance.]
As you get farther from Earth the pull of gravity is less, but it never completely goes away. However, it does get small enough that it might not matter.

In 1961 during his 108 minute flight, Yuri Gagarin, the first man in space, ate a tube of meat puree with sorrel, a tube of liver pâté and one of chocolate sauce. He also drank water from a can that had a special mouthpiece, proving that it was possible to eat and drink in microgravity.
Early foods for both Soviet and U.S. astronauts were packaged in tubes, similar to toothpaste tubes, so the astronauts could squeeze the food directly into their mouth.
Food that wouldn't work in tubes was formed into small bite-sized cubes that were coated with fat to avoid crumbs.
These cubes came in flavors including bacon, cheese, peanut butter, and fruitcake although most astronauts couldn't really tell the difference.
Since the water on-board Mercury didn't taste very good and was only available at ambient temperature, NASA included drink mix powder in vacuum-packed pouches.
On the first Mercury flight, John Glenn had only orange-flavored Tang.
And, in case you're wondering, NASA did not invent Tang. It was invented by a food scientist at General Foods who also invented Cool Whip, quick-set JELL-O, and Pop Rocks, but it was just what NASA needed for their astronauts – convenient and commercially available.
Since NASA didn't want to promote any commercial product, they packaged it for flight labeled as 'orange drink' and those rules are still used today on the International Space Station – no commercialization.
Of course, when General Foods publicized the fact that astronauts drank Tang in space, their sales really 'skyrocketed'.
The final Mercury flight had the addition of freeze-dried pot roast, but there were problems with the water dispenser so astronaut Gordon Cooper was able to eat only about a third of his pot roast.
Because missions were short, food spoilage wasn't much of an issue.

As time went by NASA gradually improved their food processing methods.
Gemini flights lasted from 2 to 14 days, so food was more of a concern.
NASA closely monitored the nutritional and caloric value of the food the astronauts ate and also kept track of which ones they chose not to eat.
Eventually, meal choices were planned and packaged according to astronaut preferences.
In addition to the tubes and cubes (which were now coated with gelatin instead of fat), Gemini flights had some freeze-dried foods such as beef and gravy, fruit, chicken with vegetables, and pudding.
These packages used a one-way spring-loaded valve at one end where the user would inject ambient temperature water.
After the food was rehydrated, the user would cut off the other end of the package, insert a tube, and squeeze the food into their mouth.
Although it was nutritious, the Gemini food system was not haute cuisine and crews complained about its taste.
During this time it was reported that Russian cosmonauts were enjoying bread, salami, jelly, roast veal, apples, oranges and even caviar.
Perhaps that’s why John Young did what he did.

Gemini 3, flown by Pilot John Young and Commander Gus Grissom, had several objectives, from testing the effect of microgravity on sea urchin eggs to attempting orbital maneuvers in a manned spacecraft that would aid the future Moon landings.
But another job was to test new space foods. Grissom and Young were sent up with dehydrated packets that were meant to be reconstituted with a water gun.
Partway through the flight, John Young pulled a corned beef sandwich out of his flight suit pocket and handed it to Grissom. After tasting it they quickly decided to stow it away because they were creating so many crumbs.
In his memoir John Young said, "Afterwards, a couple of congressmen became upset, thinking that by smuggling in the sandwich and eating part of it, Gus and I had ignored the actual space food that we were up there to evaluate, costing the country millions of dollars."
The incident sparked a review by the U.S. House of Representatives Committee on Appropriations, in which one member of Congress called it 'a $30 million sandwich' and politicians cited safety concerns about crumbs interfering with spacecraft operations.
Even though the entire sandwich incident took about 10 seconds during the 4 hour 52 minute-long flight, NASA was forced to promise that no similar incidents would happen again.
A frequently cited quote from the Congressional hearing comes from George Mueller, then NASA's associate administrator for manned spaceflight who stated, "We have taken steps to prevent any recurrences of corned-beef sandwiches in future flights."
However, corned beef did officially make it onto the menu in time for the first Shuttle flight in April 1981 that was, ironically, a mission commanded by John Young.

{{< slide n="9" img="slides/slide-009.webp" >}}
The variety of food options continued to expand for the Apollo missions.
The new availability of 150° hot water made rehydrating freeze-dried foods simpler, and produced a more appetizing result.
Starting with the second manned Apollo mission, Apollo 8, a new package called the 'spoon-bowl' was used.
It had a zip-type seal at the top that allowed the astronauts to use a spoon to scoop the food out of the bag instead of just sucking it out.
All foods that were rehydrated had some type of gravy or sauce that allowed them to stick to the spoon instead of floating around the cabin.
A new technique, thermostabilization, allowed for packaging of meats that didn't freeze-dry very well.
Thermostabilization treats food with high temperature and pressure to kill spoilage microorganisms, similar to canning but without the can.
These foods are not considered medically sterile, but rather 'commercially sterile', the same as canned goods.
As you can imagine, meat dishes that tasted almost home-made were a big hit with the astronauts.
And, in case you're wondering, freeze-dried ice cream flew in space only once, on the Apollo 7 mission in 1968.
It was too crumbly to be practical in microgravity and it was not a popular taste option.
And for those of you who might be interested, I have samples of commercially available freeze-dried ice cream here on the table that you can taste when we're finished.

{{< slide n="10" img="slides/slide-010.webp" >}}
A primary purpose of the three Skylab missions in the early 1970s was to gather physiological information on the astronauts' ability to function during periods of prolonged weightlessness.
Researchers measured the nutrient input and output of all Skylab astronauts starting 21 days before the flight, continuing throughout the flight, and for an additional 18 days afterwards. 
Another goal of Skylab was to make the living and working environment comfortable and enjoyable, and food was a large part of that.
Before their mission astronauts would taste each of 72 available foods and rate them from 1 to 9 as to which they preferred, which was then used to pack custom meals for each crew member.
Nutrient content was tightly controlled, especially salt, with each meal containing specific amounts of protein, calcium, phosphorus, sodium, magnesium, and potassium.
In order to make Skylab as 'normal' as possible, the astronauts had a table in their galley, they used regular silverware, and each had their own tray to hold their food.
The front three wells in the food tray could heat food to 150° F while the other wells stayed cool.
All the food was packed in cans, and beverage mixes were in expandable plastic bottles to which water was added.
Unlike previous missions, and even later missions, Skylab had a freezer that contained some of their food.
Although it wasn't very big, each person was allowed three frozen items in any two-day period.
All the food for the 28-day, 59-day and what became the 84-day mission were launched in the unmanned Skylab when it was first put into orbit.
Because of this, all the non-frozen food had to be shelf stable for at least a year.
Once the first crew got there and had their first meals they let Mission Control know that they weren't very happy about the food because it was so bland.
As a result, crews on Skylab 2 and 3 brought with them hot sauce, horseradish, black pepper in oil, and garlic to supplement the ketchup that was already on-board.
The third Skylab mission was originally going to be 56 days long, but scientists wanted to be able to run more experiments than would fit into that amount of time.
Since Skylab had launched with only enough food for the planned 56 days, the third crew brought with them an additional 28-day supply of food in the form of nutritionally balanced high-density food bars.
During the mission, on every third day the only thing the astronauts ate were these food bars so they could stretch the mission to 84 days.
Although the food bars provided the same nutrients and calories as the planned meals, because there was less actual food the astronauts quickly learned that they would be fairly hungry every third day.
Another thing learned from these long duration missions was that 'set diets' became monotonous.
The crew really preferred to have 'open pantry' food system where they could choose what to eat instead of rigidly planned meals.

The first four Shuttle flights used the same food packaging system as Skylab and Apollo.
Early Shuttle flights also tried using military 'Meal, Ready to Eat' rations, known as MREs.
However, NASA's doctors found that although MREs were fine for warfighters in the field they had too many calories and too much salt for astronauts to eat on a regular basis.
As on Skylab, in microgravity eating food with too much salt will cause loss of muscle mass and bone density.
Although they were available, commercial off-the-shelf freeze-dried meals had similar problems, so NASA decided to produce their own food.
By the fifth Shuttle flight NASA had developed new packaging and defined seven categories of foods, as follows:
Beverages are dehydrated drink mixes that are mostly repackaged off-the-shelf products.
Fresh Foods are foods with a two-day shelf life. For some reason, fresh foods in microgravity go bad faster than they do on Earth.
Intermediate Moisture are foods that won’t spoil as quickly as fresh foods, such as smoked meats, sausages, and dried fruit.
Natural Form foods are things such as nuts, cookies, and crackers all packaged in single servings so they will be bite-sized to avoid crumbs and leftovers.
This includes the ever-popular M&Ms which are labeled as 'candy coated chocolates' since NASA doesn't allow the use of product names.
Both Intermediate Moisture foods and Natural Form foods are packed with nitrogen to extend their shelf life.
Rehydratable Foods are dehydrated or freeze-dried foods that can be reconstituted with water, such as the Pasta with Shrimp, Broccoli au Gratin, and Rice-Krispies-with-milk shown here.
Thermostabilized Foods are prepared with heat to kill off possible spoiling agents.
The food is packed in plastic then sealed inside metallized pouches for additional protection against spoiling.
Most of these packages look the same, as you can see here with the 'Brown Rice' and 'Tofu with Hot Mustard Sauce.'
This process uses the same high heat and pressure that a standard canning process uses, it just uses multilayer pouches instead of metal cans to reduce weight and volume.
Note that Russia still uses cans instead of multilayer pouches for their astronauts.
And last we have Irradiated Meat which is also double-packed in metallized pouches
Irradiation is limited to only a few meats, including turkey slices and beef steak, because it works better than thermostabilization for meat.
Since irradiation is not approved for general food use, NASA has a special dispensation for its limited use.
As you can see from the pasta-with-shrimp label, the labels on ISS are in both English and Russian.
This was a requirement during the Shuttle-Mir program in 1995 which provided extended stays for NASA astronauts on the Mir space station allowing us to learn to live in space for longer periods of time.
Since we're using similar food systems today on the International Space Station, and we have Russian partners on the ISS, we still label all foods in both English and Russian, as well as being bar coded for traceability.

In 1984, three years after the Shuttle started flying, researchers for Coca-Cola had an idea about dispensing carbonated beverages in space to give astronauts more choices to drink and also to create a great advertising opportunity.
The company developed a can that would work in weightlessness to keep the cola fizzy without spewing out of the can.
After reviewing it, NASA agreed to let the astronauts try the device on a Shuttle flight.
When Pepsi learned of this project it also wanted to participate and developed its own container that NASA approved.
Both Coke and Pepsi were flown on the STS 51- F mission in 1985 so crew members could evaluate the dispensers and do a taste test, but the astronauts didn't reveal the results until the post-flight press briefing.
It turns out that both products tasted about as expected at room temperature since there was no refrigerator on the Shuttle.
Both cans functioned properly, but the Pepsi can dispensed a more frothy liquid which led to the astronauts playing with floating 'fizzy Pepsi balls' in microgravity.
However, they did learn there was a problem with carbonated beverages in microgravity.
When you open a can of soda on Earth the bubbles rise to the top because they are less dense than the liquid.
In microgravity, the bubbles don't rise because there's no gravity to make the heavier liquid fall and the lighter gas rise.
As a result, you end up with a stomach full of carbonated beverage that doesn't naturally separate.
The astronauts learned very quickly that when you do finally burp, it's a 'wet burp' – a mixture of liquid, gas and stomach contents, making it very unpleasant.
In the end, NASA did not add either company's product to the list of foods carried on the Shuttle.
As a result, the 'Cola Wars' continued on Earth but not in space.
————-Extra info —————
Coke invested about $250,000 dollars to adapt what looked like one of its aluminum cans to hold a laminated plastic bag filled with soda and a carbon dioxide-pressurized bladder to expel the liquid. The can was topped with a metal valve and a drinking spout. 
For its can, Pepsi modified an existing design similar to the dispensers used to package spray cheese or whipped cream. Pepsi's 'space can' also used a carbon dioxide-filled pouch to push the soda out, but instead of being pre-pressurized, it employed chemicals to produce the carbon dioxide.
The Coke can was a serious development project for use by astronauts, whereas, due to limited time Pepsi's can was not nearly as high tech and was more like a standard whipped cream can.
There was some reluctance on the part of the crew to participate in the taste test because it seemed so commercial and NASA specifically avoids commercial endorsements.
NASA, seeking to downplay the commercial nature of the payload, referred to the Coke and Pepsi cans as the 'Carbonated Beverage Container Evaluation' (CBCE) experiment and relegated the activity to whenever the crew had time between their primary experiments of advancing the study of solar, atmospheric and astro-physics.
It wasn't until NASA recast it as 'Can we use carbonated beverages in space?' that it seemed like a reasonable request and the crew agreed to be guinea pigs.
In the end neither soda was preferred, especially since it was warm and it caused 'wet burps'.

In 1985, Mexican Payload Specialist Rodolfo Neri Vela, who was going to be a crew member aboard Atlantis during the STS-61B mission, requested that tortillas be included in his food supply.
Once on orbit, his fellow crew members noticed that the tortillas, unlike regular bread, didn’t create crumbs and could be used to make sandwiches or hold other foods.
As a result, tortillas quickly became popular with astronauts on the Shuttle.
However, regular tortillas spoil after a few weeks without refrigeration.
So the JSC food lab surveyed all the tortilla-making companies in Houston and found the tortillas with the lowest water content.
Before each Shuttle flight the food lab would pack some of these fresh tortillas into flight quality packaging that the astronauts would take with them to Kennedy Space Center for launch on their Shuttle mission.
This worked well for Shuttle flights that lasted only two or three weeks.
When the International Space Station came along with longer-duration missions, the JSC food lab researched ways to extend the shelf life of the tortillas.
In the end, the best solution was for the food lab to make and package their own tortillas, resulting in a six month shelf life.
The food lab was in the tortilla-making business for several years until ISS missions started lasting even longer.
The problem was that after six months in space the JSC-made tortillas would get bitter, so the food lab started looking for other options.
Around this time Taco Bell came out with a commercial tortilla pack that was microbiologically designed to last nine months.
So NASA started buying tortillas from the same supplier that Taco Bell used and got out of the tortilla-making business.
Technology has evolved to the point that now ISS tortillas are packed in nitrogen, are mold resistant, and are shelf stable for 18 months.
Crew members use them to make breakfast burritos, hamburgers, and even peanut butter and jelly sandwiches.

Foods on the ISS are similar to those that were flown on the Shuttle.
The U.S. provides about half of the foods for the International Space Station and the other half is provided by our international partners for their astronauts, primarily Russia.
It’s not uncommon for U.S. astronauts, once they get into space, to begin trading food with the Russians and vice-versa.
As with the Shuttle, astronauts visit the JSC food lab before their mission to review the 200 different food and drink items that are available, ranking them from most-liked to least-liked.
Then the food lab organizes the foods and labels them with each astronaut's name.
The food lab makes about half of the foods from scratch, using the same ingredients we can buy at the store, but with their carefully controlled processes and recipes.
The other half are off-the-shelf items but they all have to be re-packaged with flight-qualified materials and into proper portion sizes.

{{< slide n="17" img="slides/slide-017.webp" >}}
Similar types of food, like meat, vegetables, and fruit, are all packed together in collapsible food boxes that can be folded flat for storage when they are empty.
Each astronaut can choose whatever they want to eat from the foods packaged for them. 
In addition to these foods, each person has the option to have up to nine personal preference containers (known as 'bonus boxes') with more of their favorites or other foods and up to five containers with personal drinks, all of which have to meet NASA requirements and be packaged by NASA. 
Ethnic foods are often chosen for bonus containers and each container has the crew member's name on it.
At any given time, the ISS will have Russian, American, and International astronauts on board.
Each group of astronauts sent to run the station for six to eight months, known as an 'expedition', will train together for about 2 years prior to flight.
The Russian space agency prepares the food for the Russian cosmonauts and all the food for an expedition is sent to Station together on a cargo ship before the crew launches.
Beyond the standard supply of food for each expedition, the ISS has a reserve supply of food that will support the crew for an additional 4 months.
If their work schedules on Station allow it, the crew tries to eat suppers together, especially on Fridays to maintain a sense of camaraderie.
Also, despite what might be happening on Earth, on Station they try very hard to avoid politics.
After all, if something catastrophic happens on the Space Station you could be depending on your crewmates to save your life.

In microgravity the food sometimes tastes different than it does on Earth.
On Earth the human body is constantly fighting gravity to pump fluids throughout our body.
In space, without gravity pulling it down, fluids tend to gather in our head causing congestion similar to that of a cold.
As a result, food may not taste as good as it would at home.
Luckily, this effect usually goes away as the body gets accustomed to microgravity.
Also, in microgravity the smell of food doesn't rise up to your nose but instead moves out in all directions so it's not as intense as it is on Earth.
As you can imagine, with all that equipment and other people living in close quarters, there are a lot of competing smells on ISS.
When astronauts return from an extended stay on ISS many find they have lost about 5% of their body weight.
On average, ISS astronauts eat 20 percent fewer calories than their recommended daily intake, but researchers haven't yet learned why.
Although moving around in microgravity looks effortless, working astronauts exert just as much energy as they do on the ground.
In addition, they are required to have at least two hours of exercise six days a week (using the treadmill, exercise bike or resistive exercise equipment) to keep their muscles and bones strong.
They monitor their weight using a system that measures their acceleration when a known force is applied.
When asked why they eat less in space, some have said they were too busy or just weren't hungry.
If their weight loss gets to 10% it's brought to their attention so they can change their eating habits.

{{< slide n="19" img="slides/slide-019.webp" >}}
This is the galley in Node 1 of the U.S. Segment where the astronauts prepare their meals.
There is a similar galley in the Russian Segment that the cosmonauts use and, because it's larger, that's often where everybody gathers when they all eat together.
Since things in microgravity tend to float away, food items are held to the table with strips of tape (sticky side up), binder clips, elastic straps, tethers and Velcro.
As mentioned earlier, the JSC food lab is careful about the salt content of the food they prepare.
Consequently, condiments are very popular on Station and can be added by each person according to their taste.
One of the most popular condiments is hot sauce and there are usually several different varieties on board.
Salt is available in the form of salt water (because grains of salt would just float around), and black pepper is mixed with olive oil, again because loose grains of pepper would just float around causing problems.
Of course there are many other sauces and flavorings that the JSC food lab can provide if an astronaut requests it before flight.
Rehydratable foods all have instructions about how much hot or ambient water to add and how long to wait before eating.
Thermostabilized foods, intermediate moisture foods and natural form foods are ready to eat straight out of their package.
Most prepared foods have a sauce or are thick enough that they will stick to an eating utensil long enough for the astronaut to get it into their mouth.
The astronaut's eating utensils are scissors for cutting open pouches of food, a long handled spoon for reaching down into those pouches, a fork, and a knife.
Since water is so precious they don't wash their utensils. Instead they wipe them thoroughly with disinfectant wipes.
There is no microwave oven on Station because it uses too much power and could emit radiated energy that might interfere with on-board equipment.
However, there is a warming oven where the user clamps their food to heating plates and 10 to 20 minutes later the food is hot enough to enjoy.
There are no refrigerators for storing food but there are two small chillers for drinks.
Food is packaged in single-serving portions and any leftover food that is not eaten gets thrown away.
One of the complaints from astronauts is that they are encouraged to eat all the food they open.
That's because there is no refrigeration and no way to preserve leftovers, so any uneaten food will spoil.
As you can imagine, Station generates a lot of trash which is stowed on board until an unmanned supply vehicle arrives, which happens about every two to four months.
Once this vehicle is unloaded, it is then reloaded with trash including any uneaten food that has been accumulating (and spoiling) since the last unmanned-vehicle-loaded-with-trash departed.
After undocking from Station this vehicle full of trash will burn up as it re-enters the atmosphere.

{{< slide n="20" img="slides/slide-020.webp" >}}
The resupply ships carry equipment, food, water, and other supplies, as well as a limited amount of fresh food which is a special treat for the crew who normally eat only packaged food.
Fresh food spoils fairly quickly on Station due to high accelerations during launch and poor storage conditions, so the astronauts eat it quickly.
As missions grow longer, and especially with the long range goal of going to Mars which is a 2+ year trip, the idea of growing food in space has become more important.
There have been several experiments to grow food on ISS, and recently they grew Hatch hot chiles that the astronauts were allowed to taste.
They were thrilled to put fresh chiles on their tacos.
Many astronauts have reported they missed the textures and freshness of fresh fruits and vegetables during their time on Station.
If a SpaceX resupply vehicle is scheduled to carry the freezer to Station for returning biological samples, it can carry ice cream to the astronauts as a special treat.
In case you were wondering, alcohol is not allowed on Station, primarily because it's a contaminant that causes problems with the US Segment's water recycling system.
Although they are not supposed to, some Russians have been known to have alcohol in their segment of Station.

Since we've been talking about food so much, we should talk about what happens after the crew processes that food.
Here's a view of the toilet on ISS, with the privacy curtain pulled back.
The ISS toilet uses vacuum airflow to capture solid and liquid waste from astronauts.
In this view the white lid to the toilet seat is closed, but it's opened before use.
Astronauts don't really sit on this seat, they sort of float above it to do their business.
Solid waste is sucked down through the seat and captured in plastic bags that are sealed and stored in the silver cylinder you see here until it can be loaded onto a supply vehicle along with the rest of the trash.
For liquid waste, the astronaut would unhook the gray hose with black stripes from the wall, make sure the vacuum airflow is running, and capture liquid waste via a funnel on the end of the hose.
And, yes, there are different funnel attachments for men and women.
The urine from the toilet, along with all the moisture from the air, is captured and processed through a very complex recycling system that is 98% efficient and produces drinking water that is cleaner than we get out of our taps here on Earth.
That water is then used by the crew for drinking, food preparation, washing, etc. – about one gallon per day per person.
Recycling the water is much more cost effective than sending large amounts of fresh water to Station on resupply missions.
And, as the astronauts like to say, “Yesterday’s coffee becomes tomorrow’s coffee.”
  
————-Extra info —————
NASA decided to develop a water recycling system because, in the early days when the Shuttle was the only way to get cargo to and from ISS, it was very expensive to send water to Station.
Each astronaut uses about a gallon of water per day.
Water weighs 8 lb per gallon.
It cost about $20,000 to transport one pound of cargo to the ISS on the Shuttle.
Thus, each gallon of water would cost $160,000 to send to Station.
As a result, it was much more cost effective to develop a water recycling system.
In the Russian portion of ISS they recycle the moisture from the air, but not their urine.
Their urine is captured in bags and disposed of with the other trash in the Progress resupply vehicles.
If the U.S. astronauts want to, they can get bags of urine from the Russians, connect it to the U.S. water recycling system, and generate clean water for the U.S. astronauts.
But they generally don't do that because they are usually too busy.
Since the water recycling system is only 98% efficient, they have to occasionally send up more water.
A recent resupply mission sent to Station included 40 gallons of water for the U.S. astronauts, which would last about 3 months.

Speaking of coffee, earlier I told you that you can't use a coffee cup in microgravity except in very specific circumstances. Let's talk about that.
In 2008, astronaut Don Petit realized that the surface tension of liquid in microgravity causes it to stick to surfaces.
As an experiment he took two plastic sheets from a notebook and created a microgravity coffee cup.
After returning to Earth he worked with Portland State University to develop a 3D-printed cup that would work in microgravity.
Because of its shape, surface tension and capillary flow cause the liquid to flow into the narrow channel towards the opening of the cup.
In 2015 ESA astronaut Samantha Cristoforetti used an experimental version of the cup to drink espresso from the experimental espresso machine that had been installed on ISS.

There's a lot of discussion about going to Mars.
If we want to go to Mars, it's a really long trip.
Mars and Earth orbit the sun at different speeds and are close together only every 26 months.
Going to the Moon takes about four days.
Going to Mars will take from 6 to 9 months to get there, then you may spend a couple of months on Mars, then 9 months to a year to get home.
So you'll be gone for 2 to 2 1/2 years.
Food for a trip like this has significant challenges.
We can make food shelf-stable for a long time but the quality will not be very good.
We can stop biological and microbial growth but we can't prevent natural chemical changes in food that result in degraded texture, flavor, and some loss of some nutrients.
After a period of eating degraded food, the astronauts on a trip to Mars will likely eat only enough food to survive and not really enough to thrive.
That's a big challenge, because NASA wants high-performing crew members throughout the entire 2 1/2 year mission.
It's possible we could pre-position some food, that is, send it to Mars before the crew gets there.
But if the crew is eating this food on the way back to Earth, that food might already be 4-5 years old.
If we used slower and cheaper missions to pre-position the food, then it would be even older.
We know that if we store shelf-stable foods in a cool environment it will slow down the degradation.
But we can't depend on a refrigerator to preserve the food, because if the fridge fails we'll lose our food.
For a 2 1/2 year mission to Mars the food for a crew of six would weigh about 20,000 lb if we use the food system we use for ISS.
That's 10 tons of food to send to Mars!
Several companies are working on self-contained ways to grow food for long duration space flights.
As you can see, we have a lot of challenges and technology development ahead of us if we want to go to Mars.
