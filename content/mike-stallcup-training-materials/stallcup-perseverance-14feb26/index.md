I'd like to start by giving you a little historical perspective.
Here we can see the relative size and complexity of the various rovers that NASA has sent to Mars. Sojourner (at the lower left) was the size of a microwave oven. In 1997 [designed for 30 days, lasted 83 days] it proved that a rover could be used to explore Mars instead of having only stationary landers as we had done up to that point.
In 2004 the twin golf-cart-sized rovers, Spirit [survived 6 years] and Opportunity [survived 14 years], discovered evidence that the planet once hosted running water before becoming a frozen desert.
Curiosity and Perseverance are similar to each other with a few differences.
In 2012 [13 years so far] the car-sized Curiosity rover landed and discovered that its landing site, Gale Crater, hosted a lake billions of years ago, as well as an environment that could have supported microbial life.
Perseverance landed in 2021 [4 years so far] and it aims to find out if there are signs of past microbial life on Mars.
China is the only other country to land a rover on Mars. The Zhurong rover, part of the Tianwen-1 mission, landed on Mars in May 2021. It was expected to operate for 90 days but survived for about a year before dust built up on its solar panels to the point that it could no longer charge its batteries.

{{< slide n="6" img="slides/slide-006.webp" >}}
Perseverance landed in 'Yez-A-Roh' Crater, which is sometimes incorrectly pronounced 'Jez-er-oh'. The name Yez-A-Roh is a Slavic word meaning 'lake'.
Yez-A-Roh Crater is an impact basin about 28 mi [45 km] wide and 1600 ft [490 m] deep. About 3 to 4 billion years ago this basin contained a lake whose level fluctuated dramatically. A river flowed into this lake carrying sediment that formed a large delta, which scientists believe may contain organic molecules and other potential signs of microbial life. That's where Perseverance started exploring.
————-Extra info —————
'Jezero' is a slavic word meaning 'lake.' Pronounce it 'Yez-A-Roh' and roll the 'R'.
The delta required a period of one to ten million years to form, so life may have developed here.
The floor of the crater was made of two types of igneous rock – one that formed deep underground from magma, the other from volcanic activity at the surface.
Carbonate and sulfate minerals were also detected which means that the rocks had been altered by water.
Clay minerals have been detected in and around the crater. Clays form in the presence of water, so this area probably once held water, and perhaps life, in ancient times.

{{< slide n="7" img="slides/slide-007.webp" >}}
Now let's compare Curiosity and Perseverance. The two rovers have somewhat different missions. 
Curiosity's four goals were:
First: Determine whether life ever arose on Mars. Were environmental conditions and chemical elements able to support life?
Next: Characterize the ancient and current climate of Mars. An earlier thicker, wetter atmosphere may have provided better environmental conditions for supporting microbial life. Curiosity found nutrients and energy sources that microbes could have used, and established that Mars had regions that could have been friendly to life in the ancient past.
Third: Characterize the ancient and current geology of Mars. Look for evidence of rocks that formed in the presence of water.
Finally: Prepare for human exploration. Land a heavy payload and characterize radiation levels for future human exposure. 
Perseverance also has four goals:
First: Look for habitability. Identify geology of past environments capable of supporting microbial life.
Next: Look for biosignatures. Seek signs of possible past microbial life (called 'biomarkers') in those habitable environments, particularly in special rocks known to preserve signs of life over time.
Third: Cache samples. Collect rock cores and soil samples, store some aboard Perseverance and also store some on the Martian surface. By the way, the soil on any object in space other than Earth is referred to as 'regolith'.
Finally: Prepare for humans.
Characterize the atmosphere and dust to understand the possible effects on humans and equipment
Take weather measurements to validate global atmospheric models
Test oxygen production from the Martian atmosphere. By August of 2023 one of the science instruments on Perseverance, called Moxie, had run 22 times and successfully generated twice as much oxygen as scientists had expected.
————- Extra Info —————-
MOXIE - Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment
Produced a total of 122 g [4.3 oz] of oxygen — roughly the amount a small dog breathes in 10 hours, and twice as much as scientists thought MOXIE could make during its tenure. 
At its most efficient, MOXIE produced 12 g [0.4 oz] of oxygen every hour.

{{< slide n="9" img="slides/slide-009.webp" >}}
After a 7 month trip through space, both Curiosity and Perseverance used a 'sky crane' maneuver to land gently on Mars.
Years earlier, Spirit and Opportunity had used airbags to surround the rovers as they bounced to a landing.
However, Curiosity and Perseverance were five times heavier than Spirit and Opportunity. Retro rockets strong enough to land these two heavy rovers on the surface would have created craters that the rovers might get stuck in. Thus, the sky crane idea was born.
The beauty of this is that the landing system delivers the rover on its wheels, ready to roll, and the retro rockets are kept fairly far from the surface.
Since Mars is so far away, the landing process had to be completely autonomous without any ground intervention.
The vehicles had onboard cameras, sensors, and computers to recognize the pre-defined landing zones and control the seven-minute descent and landing.
This was known as the ‘seven minutes of terror’ because it was all autonomous.
Ground controllers on Earth could not actively manage the landing because, at that time, it took more than 11 minutes for radio signals to travel between Earth and Mars.
They could only monitor what happened during the seven minutes it took to enter the atmosphere and land.
The vehicle entered the Martian atmosphere going 12,500 mph and seven minutes later the rover would be on the ground.
Happily, all systems worked. Both Curiosity and, nine years later, Perseverance landed safely.

Now let's compare Curiosity and Perseverance.
First of all, they are both still operating and performing good science on Mars.
They both have self-driving capabilities, but Perseverance has a much more advanced system. The rover drivers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Lab in California, where the mission is managed, give Perseverance specific stops along a route, but it chooses how to get there and how to avoid hazards along that path.
Perseverance can perform this routing while it is moving, whereas Curiosity takes longer and has to plan its entire route before starting out each time.
The two rovers are about the size and weight of a small car.
Both include a radioisotope thermoelectric generator, which I will call an RTG since it's easier to say. <Point to the RTG, white fins in box at the rear of the deck>
This converts the heat from decaying plutonium-238 into electricity and is expected to last at least 14 years, declining by a few percent each year.
Both Curiosity and Perseverance were required to operate for at least one Mars year, which is 687 Earth days or 1.9 Earth years. 
As of today [11/5/25], Curiosity has been on Mars over 13 Earth years and Perseverance has been there over  4-1/2 years.
Both have two rechargeable lithium-ion batteries that provide extra power when needed.
Excess heat from the RTG is used to keep the vehicle systems warm, which is a good thing because temperatures on Mars where Perseverance is located range from a low of about -120 Fahrenheit [-84 Celsius] at night in winter to a high of about +40 F [+4 C] at noon in summer. The average temperature is about -67 F [-55 C].
In addition, winds of up to 56 mph [90 km/hr] have been recorded by the on-board weather-measuring science instrument.
Both rovers have two computers, a primary and a backup, and two forms of radio communications.
X-band provides low bandwidth signals directly to and from Earth and is used mostly to send commands to the rover each morning.
The UHF high bandwidth signal is used to relay images and science data to Earth via five Mars orbiting vehicles, including two from the European Space Agency.
It generally takes anywhere from 5 to 20 minutes for a radio signal to travel the distance between Mars and Earth, depending on the positions of the planets.

Curiosity is 2.9 m [9.5 ft] long, about 2.7 m [9 ft] wide and about 2.1 m [7 ft] tall [9'6" long x 8'10" wide x 7'3" tall]. Perseverance is about 13 cm [5 in] longer, but the same height and width.
Curiosity weighs about 907 kg [2000 lb] [1,982 lb, 180 lb scientific instruments] and Perseverance weighs about 1043 kg [2300 lb] [2,260 lb, 278 lb more].
The RTG generates 110 Watts of power [2.5 KWh/day].
Both have two rechargeable lithium-ion batteries [42 Ah each] that provide extra power when needed.
Perseverance can require as much as 900 W during some science operations.

As of today [1/15/25], Curiosity has been on Mars 4537 days, about 12.5 Earth years, and Perseverance has been there 1428 days, almost 4 years.

{{< slide n="12" img="slides/slide-012.webp" videoFile="videos/slide-012.mp4" >}}
Rovers wouldn't be any good without wheels, and these rovers have some pretty special wheels and suspension.
Both have six wheels, that are all powered, in an arrangement called a 'rocker-bogie' that keeps all the wheels in contact with the ground as they roll over uneven terrain.
The wheels on both sides of the vehicle are connected to each other so that when one set goes up, such as when they go over a rock, the wheels on the other side are pushed down which raises that side of the vehicle, keeping it fairly level.
The four corner wheels are independently steerable and all the wheels have titanium springs and spokes.
They can drive over rocks and through depressions almost the size of the wheels.
Curiosity's wheels are 20 inch [51 cm] in diameter, with Perseverance having slightly larger wheels.
Curiosity's wheels have an aluminum skin the thickness of about 7 sheets of paper. By being this thin the skin can flex, similar to a rubber tire but more durable.
After several years of wear Curiosity's wheels showed substantial damage from Martian rocks, so when Perseverance was designed it got similar wheels that are narrower, slightly larger in diameter, with aluminum skin twice as thick and with twice as many cleats, which are actually called 'grousers'. 
Perseverance's average speed is 167 ft/hr (0.03 mph), max 500 ft/hr (0.09 mph)
Curiosity's average speed is 98 ft/hr (0.02 mph), max speed is 300 ft/hr (0.06 mph)
For reference, walking speed is about 2.5 mph.
So Perseverance's maximum speed is about 1/30 our average walking speed.
It's much more important for the rovers to safely get where they are going than it is for them to get there quickly. 
————- Extra Info —————-
They can drive over rocks and through depressions as large as 41 cm [16 in]
Curiosity wheels: 0.76 mm [0.030 in] skin with 24 grousers (cleats), 51 cm [20 inch] diam.
Perseverance wheels: 1.5 mm [0.060 in] skin with 48 grousers (cleats), 53 cm [20.7 in] diam.
Perseverance has a second computer dedicated entirely to image processing, eliminating the need to pause to decide its next move. This allows ground controllers to tell Perseverance where to go and it can decide the best route using its on-board cameras and computers. Previous rovers would have to stop frequently to plan their next route.

{{< slide n="13" img="slides/slide-013.webp" >}}
After 13 years of wear, Curiosity's wheels are showing some damage.
This is caused by several things, but most importantly it's from sharp wind-eroded rocks that are sticking out of the ground.
Although Curiosity's designers knew there were sharp rocks on Mars, they didn't know that sometimes they were deeply embedded in the soil.
No place we've ever been on Mars before has these kinds of embedded pointy rocks.
That's why the wheels on Perseverance have a skin that's twice as thick as Curiosity's.
The rover drivers now choose routes that will take Curiosity over more benign terrain.
By the way, I mentioned earlier that the engineers at JPL left a little of themselves on Curiosity.
Each wheel on Curiosity has 12 odometer holes machined into it so it will leave a distinctive pattern of dots and dashes in the sand as it travels.
This allows the rover drivers to look back at this pattern and calculate the amount of slippage the wheels experienced.
It turns out these dots and dashes are actually Morse Code and they spell out 'JPL'.
And, no, Perseverance does not have any odometer holes in its wheels.

{{< slide n="14" img="slides/slide-014.webp" >}}
Several of the systems on Perseverance are similar, but upgraded, versions of those on Curiosity.
For instance, Curiosity has 17 cameras, but Perseverance has 23 cameras as well as two microphones, which by the way are the first working microphones we've ever had on Mars.
Both rovers have seven science instruments, but they are different instruments. Perseverance's instruments benefit from technology advancements that occurred in the nine years between the two rovers.
They both have a laser that can vaporize a spot on a rock up to 23 ft [7 m] away then analyze the spectra of the resulting plasma to determine the makeup of that rock.
If scientists want to investigate the rock further, Curiosity will roll closer then drill into the rock and use its instruments to analyze it. Similarly, Perseverance has instruments to analyze samples but it can also detect the presence of organic compounds in rocks and regolith either up close or from a distance.
Perseverance also has another first for Mars – a ground penetrating radar that can provide unparalleled detail of rock layers up to 50 ft [15 m] underground.

{{< slide n="15" img="slides/slide-015.webp" >}}
When Perseverance landed on Mars it was carrying a little helper, a small helicopter called Ingenuity which became the first aircraft to achieve powered, controlled flight on another planet.
It successfully flew in the extremely thin Martian atmosphere which is only about 1% as dense as Earth's atmosphere, the equivalent of being at 25 miles altitude on Earth.
For reference, commercial jetliners on Earth fly at around 5 to 7 miles altitude.
Ingenuity's prime mission was to perform just five flights to prove its ability to fly on Mars.
Once that was accomplished, the team decided to use Ingenuity as an aerial scout for Perseverance's scientists and rover drivers, to access terrain that was difficult to reach and send back pictures for possible investigations.
As with Perseverance, ground controllers tell Ingenuity where to go and how to get there. Its on-board sensors and computers are used to evaluate its surroundings to be sure it stays on course.
It has solar panels that charge lithium-ion batteries to provide enough energy for one 90-second flight per Martian day as well as keeping it warm during the night.
The two carbon fiber blades are 47 in [119 cm] long and rotate at 2400 rpm. They provide enough lift to support the helicopter's 1.5 lb [680 g] in Mars' gravity, which is 38% that of Earth's gravity. On Earth Ingenuity weighs about 4 lb [1.8 kg].
Since Mars and Earth orbit the Sun at different speeds, they are only close together every 26 months. Their separation ranges from 35 to 250 M mi [56 to 400 M km]. That's roughly 150 to 1000 times the distance from Earth to our Moon.
Because of this distance, radio signals from the helicopter are relayed to Earth through Perseverance and several Mars orbiters, which can take from 5 to 20 minutes one-way depending on the positions of Mars and Earth.
Originally, Ingenuity's flights were to be no farther than 980 ft [300 m] and as high as 15 ft [4.6 m], but these numbers were extended as the operators got more experience with the helicopter.
Eventually it exceeded expectations; flying twice as far, three times as high and twice as long as designed.
Flight durations typically last only 2 to 3 minutes and are limited by battery power and to avoid high motor temperatures.
Ingenuity's last flight, its 72nd, was on Jan. 18, 2024, meaning it functioned for 1005 days, or 2 3/4 years. Not bad for a test vehicle that was originally going to make only five flights.
Its last flight was cut short when it flew over a stretch of Martian sands that were so nondescript that it was difficult for the helicopter to identify landscape features for navigation.
When it tried to land it was still traveling horizontally, which caused it to pitch over such that the propellers cracked.
As a result, the tips of three rotor blades broke off and a fourth blade broke completely off.
Due to this damage it was no longer capable of flight.
<Discuss photos and play the video where you can see that half of one blade is missing.>
In the end Ingenuity accumulated 128 flying minutes and flew over 10.5 miles [17.0 km].
Ingenuity's final resting place has been nicknamed 'The Valinor Hills' after the fictional location in J.R.R. Tol-keen's [Tolkien's] 'The Lord of the Rings.' Valinor was a part of the Undying Lands which the elves knew as a place of eternal refuge and rest.
Although it is now a static observing station, Ingenuity still beams weather and avionics test data to the Perseverance rover about once a week, and will continue to do so until Perseverance travels too far away. Once Perseverance is out of range Ingenuity will store the data in its on-board memory which can hold about 20 years' worth of daily data.
The weather information might benefit future explorers of the Red Planet. The avionics data is already proving useful to engineers working on future designs of aircraft and other vehicles for the Red Planet.
Whenever humanity revisits Valinor Hills — either with a rover, a new aircraft, or future astronauts — Ingenuity will be waiting with its last gift of data.

{{< slide n="16" img="slides/slide-016.webp" >}}
One of Perseverance's primary goals is to take samples of Martian rock, regolith, and air for eventual return to Earth for analysis.
Rock samples are taken with a core drill as shown in the center photo, then stored in ultra-clean sample tubes.
Regolith samples use a different drill bit but are stored in sample tubes the same as rock samples.
Of the 43 tubes Perseverance brought to Mars, 38 are for collecting samples, and 5 are 'witness tubes' designed to document the cleanliness of its sampling system throughout the mission.
Three witness tubes capturing Mars air have already been sealed and 30 sample tubes have also been sealed.
Nine of those sample tubes and one witness tube were deposited on the surface of Mars in a 'sample depot.'
Eight of the nine have duplicate samples stored on Perseverance.
The bottom image shows the 10 tubes at the sample depot on the surface of Mars.
Scientists are confident that the sample tubes left on the Martian surface will not be covered with dust, even years after they were dropped by Perseverance. Although there are winds, and even dust devils, on Mars they don't actually carry much dust because the atmosphere is so thin. And the locations of the 10 sample tubes are very well known so it should be easy to find them in the future.
Eight sample tubes and two witness samples remain to be used.
The samples that have already been collected and sealed cannot be opened by Perseverance.
Starting with Sample 29, the sample tubes are not being sealed, just stored on-board Perseverance in a manner that their contents won't be lost or contaminated.
This gives scientists the option to dump out already-collected samples if something really interesting were to come along after all the samples were taken.

Before a rock core is drilled, the surface is abraded and inspected closely.
This enables the science team to see what’s beneath the weathered, dusty surface of a rock before deciding to drill a core sample that would be stored in one of the mission’s titanium sample tubes.
Sometimes the scientists abrade a rock and just use the on-board instruments to examine it and learn about the rock without drilling out a core sample.

It also shows where some of the samples were taken.
Perseverance has completed four science campaigns, all designed to investigate rocks in and around the areas of Yez-er-roh Crater where water used to be.
It's fifth campaign, to travel out of the crater and up to the rim, was completed in December of last year [12/10/24].
It's now exploring the area near the rim outside the crater.
So far Perseverance has traveled over 23 miles, which is quite an accomplishment for a vehicle whose average speed is about 1/30 of our average walking speed.

It needs to avoid terrain that would tilt it more than 30 degrees.
It traveled out of the crater and up to the rim gaining 500 m (1640 ft) in elevation.
Yez-er-roh Crater was formed when something large struck Mars, causing material from deep below the surface to be thrown up onto the rim and beyond.
Scientists hope to find rocks from Mars' ancient past to help them understand how Mars developed.

{{< slide n="19" img="slides/slide-019.webp" >}}
Astrobiologists use a seven-step scale called the Confidence of Life Detection, or the CoLD Scale, to research whether a sample could indicate life.
Step 1 is to detect possible signals.
Among the things that Perseverance has found are rock samples that confirm the presence of habitable environments. 
Although scientists believe these samples are their best chance of finding signs of ancient Martian life, the rocks must be returned to Earth because that level of detailed analysis can't be done with the instruments on board Perseverance.
As Perseverance explores beyond the crater's rim scientists hope to find rocks that might help them understand Martian geologic history.
Or maybe it will find rocks that could be from habitable ancient environments that have never been examined up close before.

{{< slide n="20" img="slides/slide-020.webp" >}}
This recently collected sample, named 'St. Pauls Bay', features hundreds of millimeter-size dark gray spheres, some of which have tiny pinholes.
This was found on the rim of the Jezero Crater where Perseverance has been exploring since December 2024.
These formations can arise from volcanic processes, such as the rapid cooling of molten rock droplets during an eruption, or from meteorite impacts where vaporized rocks condense to form spherules.
Once scientists determine how these were formed it will provide more insight into Mars’ past geologic history.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide n="21" img="slides/slide-021.webp" >}}
In June 2024 a sample from the Cheyava Falls outcrop called 'Sapphire Canyon' made big news.
A recently released peer-reviewed paper discusses the results of the detailed analysis performed since then.
Cheyava Falls is located just below the crater's rim in an ancient river valley that provided water to the Jezero lake.
Scientists used data from Perseverance's instruments and the pictures of Sample 25 (shown here) that were taken last year, to thoroughly analyze tiny nodules that they called 'poppy seeds' and reaction fronts that they called 'leopard spots'.
Chemical analysis revealed that these millimeter-scale structures are highly enriched in iron-phosphate and iron-sulfide minerals.
Both these minerals are associated with life on Earth.
One is often found around decomposing organic matter on Earth, while the other is produced by certain microbes.
These minerals may have formed on the rock when ancient microbes generated energy with chemical reactions that turned the rock from red to white while leaving a dark biosignature ring.
But chemical reactions not related to life can also produce these minerals under certain conditions.
In addition, Perseverance detected organic molecules in this rock, so by having these in the same rock scientists consider this to be a potential biosignature.
Importantly, organic compounds can also arrive by meteorites, or form without biology.
Work is continuing on this sample and scientists are looking into other possible explanations for these features that wouldn't involve microbes.
Fortunately, Perseverance has already drilled a core sample from this outcrop and has it safely stored on-board.
As with other samples, the only way for the true origin of these structures to be determined is by returning the samples to Earth.
So far, all the findings from Perseverance are considered 'Step 1' on the CoLD Scale.
And that's why the Mars 2020 program includes a Sample Return Mission.
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide n="22" img="slides/slide-022.webp" >}}
Once Perseverance fills all its sample tubes, we need to get them back to Earth for analysis using much larger machines than we can send to Mars, such as electron microscopes and mass spectrometers.
The original plan, as illustrated here, was to have a sample-retriever lander carry a rocket to the surface of Mars.
Perseverance would load the samples into that rocket which would eventually get them back to Earth.
Two Sample Recovery Helicopters, similar to Ingenuity, would have been used as a backup plan to pick up the tubes from the sample depot if something happened to Perseverance.
In 2024 an independent study found this approach would be too expensive and take too long.
The current plan is for NASA to simultaneously evaluate two new architectures to deliver the sample-retriever lander to Mars. This will encourage competition and innovation, as well as cost and schedule savings.
One approach will use a 'sky crane' similar to that used to land Curiosity and Perseverance.
The other approach will use an as-yet-unspecified, commercial heavy-lift vehicle—most likely some variant of the massive rockets that are being developed by companies such as SpaceX and Blue Origin.
Both approaches would still transfer the samples from Perseverance to the Sample Container inside the Mars Ascent Vehicle rocket which will be onboard the lander.
Once all the samples were loaded aboard, the Ascent Vehicle would launch and go into orbit.
Waiting in Mars orbit would be another vehicle, called the Earth Return Obiter, that will be provided by the European Space Agency.
In orbit the Ascent Vehicle would release the Sample Container into space which the Earth Return Obiter would then capture and take back to Earth.
The two Sample Recovery Helicopters would not be included in the new plans.
Separately, Lockheed Martin has proposed their own mission to return these samples from Mars for a fixed-price of $3 billion.
In addition to NASA's Sample Return Mission, China is planning to launch a mission to Mars in 2028 that would return rock and soil samples to Earth by 2031. However, this will be a sample from only one location, unlike Perseverance that will have 38 samples from different locations.
As of today [5Nov25], NASA plans to select a revised architecture by mid-2026 aiming for a more affordable design.
It remains to be seen whether or not funding for NASA's Sample Return Mission will be continued.
