Display: Great Observatories Hall - 10 min. 
The Story 
NASA has created several space-borne observatories to conduct astronomical studies over 
different wavelengths (Hubble - visible; Chandra - X-ray; Compton - gamma ray; Spitzer - 
infrared; Webb - infrared). By using multiple observatories astronomers can combine data about 
an object at different wavelengths to enhance their understanding. 
Big Ideas 
• Hubble Space Telescope: 1990–Present (36 years as of 2026) 
- Observes mostly in visible light, some near-infrared (IR), some near-ultraviolet (UV) 
- Orbits Earth every 95 minutes 
- Has one working gyroscope and one spare 
• Compton Gamma Ray Observatory: 1991–2000 (9 years) 
- Collected data on some of the most violent high-energy processes in the Universe 
• Chandra X-Ray Observatory: 1999–Present (27 years as of 2026) 
- Takes images similar to Hubble but captures X-rays instead of visible light 
- Observes things like black holes, supernova remnants, and collisions between galaxies 
- Orbits the Earth in a highly elliptical orbit every 64 hours 
- When farthest away it's about 1/3 the distance to the Moon 
- Operates only outside Earth's radiation belts, about 55 hours of each orbit 
- Has two sets of four nested hollow cylindrical glass mirrors 
- The large lighted hollow glass cylinder on display is a spare mirror blank that could have 
been ground and polished to be used for flight if one of the flight mirrors had been damaged 
during manufacturing 
- The mirror wall is 1 inch thick and the inside of the cylinder would be the mirrored surface 
• Spitzer Space Telescope: 2003–2020 (17 years) 
- Spitzer was an infrared telescope (similar to James Webb Space Telescope) but it had a solid 
mirror much smaller than the mirror on JWST 
- It operated for 17 years and revealed objects that previously couldn't be studied such as 
stellar nurseries, centers of galaxies and newly forming solar systems 
- It laid the groundwork for the types of things that JWST has been imaging 
• James Webb Space Telescope (JWST): 2021–Present (5 years as of 2026) 
- JWST is an infrared telescope with a mirror made of 18 segments that all work together to 
act as one big mirror that is 21 ft across 
- This segmented mirror has 5.5 times more collecting area than Hubble’s and 45 times more 
than Spitzer’s

Display: Artemis+Human Landing System (HLS) - 15 min. 
The Story 
The goal of the Artemis program is to send people back to the Moon to establish a sustainable 
presence and explore more of the lunar surface than ever before. The first flight, Artemis I 
(uncrewed) went around the Moon on a 25 day mission in Nov. 2022. Artemis II carried four 
crew on a 10 day mission around the Moon in April 2026. The recently revised plan (Feb. 2026) 
for Artemis III is to launch into low Earth orbit (LEO) in 2027 and dock with one or more of the 
lunar landers that will also be in LEO. In 2028 Artemis IV will fly the Orion crew module to the 
Moon, then dock with a lunar lander that will be orbiting the Moon waiting for Orion. Two of the 
crew will stay aboard Orion and two of the crew will land on the Moon near the lunar South 
Pole. 
Big Ideas 
• Artemis consists of the Space Launch System (SLS) rocket (1/10 scale SLS model on display 
here), the Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) upper stage (not in our display) and the 
Orion crew capsule (1/20 scale Orion model on display here) 
- SLS uses two solid rocket boosters (SRBs - the two white rockets) and four RS-25 engines 
mounted on the bottom of the Core Stage (the central orange part), which holds liquid 
hydrogen and liquid oxygen as fuel for the four engines 
- The Orion crew module sits at the top of the Core Stage, covered by the curved fairing that's 
just below the Launch Abort System (LAS) (the little rocket on top) 
- Below Orion, in the white cylinder above the Core Stage, sits the ICPS 
- Although we don't have a model of ICPS on display, it is the upper stage that will send Orion 
to the Moon 
- The LAS rocket is attached to the top of Orion and is the escape system for the crew 
- If something bad happens during launch or ascent, the LAS will pull Orion away from the 
vehicle and Orion's parachutes will open up saving the the crew's lives 
• Flight process 
- All four engines and both SRBs are required for launch 
- After two minutes the boosters burn out, separate and drop into the ocean 
- The LAS also comes off around this time and drops into the ocean 
- The four Core Stage engines burn for another six minutes to reach Low Earth Orbit 
- Once in orbit, Orion and its attached ICPS separate from Core Stage which drops into the 
atmosphere and burns up 
- ICPS fires to raise their orbit, then separates from Orion and drops into the atmosphere and 
ocean 
- Orion then fires its main engine and heads to the Moon 
- When Orion returns to Earth the white lower portion of the vehicle, the Service Module, 
separates and burns up in the atmosphere

- The silver part of Orion is where the crew sits and it has a heat shield that's hidden behind

- The heat shield protects the astronauts as they re-enter, the parachutes deploy, Orion splashes 
down in the ocean, then the US Navy brings Orion and the crew back to shore 
• On Artemis I, Orion (uncrewed) went around the Moon on a 25 day mission in Nov. 2022 
• Artemis II carried four crew on a 10 day mission around the Moon in April 2026 
- This flight tested the spacecraft’s systems, such as the new life support system, with crew 
aboard for the first time in the actual environment of deep space 
- The crew also took many images of the far side of the Moon and observed a solar eclipse 
- It took about four days to reach the Moon, about 3 hours to swing around the back side of 
the Moon, and about four days to return to Earth on a 'free-return trajectory' 
- No significant on-board rocket firings were needed because this trajectory uses Earth’s 
gravity to pull Orion home 
- This way the astronauts can return home safely even if there are problems with the engine in 
Orion 
• Artemis III is currently planned to launch into low Earth orbit (LEO) in 2027 and dock with 
one or more of the planned lunar landing vehicles 
- Orion is not designed to land on the Moon, so we need a second vehicle – a lunar lander 
- NASA has contracted with SpaceX to use their Starship as a lunar lander for the first two 
lunar landings 
- A team led by Blue Origin is also developing a lunar lander called Blue Moon 
- Here in the museum you can see a 1/12 scale model of the SpaceX Starship (the large white 
rocket with a horizontal black stripe) 
- We do not have a model of the Blue Moon lander 
- By docking Orion with a lunar lander in LEO we will verify the docking process and work 
out any issues close to home instead of having the first docking occur at the Moon, as was 
previously planned 
• Artemis IV will launch in 2028 and will carry four crew to the Moon 
- Before the launch of Artemis IV an uncrewed lunar lander will be sent to the Moon and will 
be waiting in orbit 
- Once Orion reaches lunar orbit it will dock with the lander, two astronauts will take the 
lander to the surface while the other two astronauts will remain aboard Orion orbiting the 
Moon 
- After a few days (TBD) the two astronauts on the Moon will use the lander to return to 
Orion, then all four astronauts will return to Earth aboard Orion 
- The empty lander could be sent into a heliocentric (sun-centered) disposal orbit if it's not 
designed to return to Earth 
- New technologies required for Artemis IV that need to be developed: SpaceX Starship, 
SpaceX’s low-Earth-orbit propellant depot to fuel Starship, Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander 
and their system to get it to the Moon, and the new spacesuits for use on the Moon 
• Artemis V is planned to include a lunar landing but as of June 2026 details are still sketchy 
• Having multiple landers with different designs provides NASA with options, similar to the way 
NASA has multiple launch suppliers for crew and cargo to ISS 
• The long range plan is to develop a crewed base on the Moon over the next eight years 
- Unmanned cargo and exploration missions will be sent first 
- Multiple missions will gradually build up the capabilities on the Moon 
- Eventually we will have a semi-permanent presence on the Moon

Display: ISS Water Recovery and Food - 15 min. 
The Water Recovery Story 
When NASA was designing the International Space Station it became clear that it would be more 
cost effective to recycle the liquid waste to produce potable water rather than constantly shipping 
up large amounts of fresh water for the crew. Part of this process involves capturing urine from 
the ISS toilet. 
Big Ideas - Water Recycling 
• The cost to send something to ISS currently runs about $5,000 per lb; on the Shuttle it was 
about $20,000 per lb 
• Each astronaut uses about a gallon of water per day which weighs 8 lb 
• Thus 8 lb x $5,000 = $40,000 per astronaut per day if we used only fresh water 
• The WRS in the US Segment of ISS is a very complex mechanical and chemical system 
• It collects urine, moisture from the air that the crew breathes out, and evaporated water from 
washcloths/towels then produces water that is cleaner than we get from our taps here on Earth 
with an efficiency of 98% 
• Earth's water recycling system, where we get our drinking water, works the same way but it's 
just much larger and slower 
• The astronauts like to say, “On ISS yesterday’s coffee becomes tomorrow’s coffee” 
Big Ideas - Waste Management 
• The ISS toilet uses vacuum airflow to capture solid and liquid waste from astronauts 
• The astronaut turns on the airflow and uses a hose with a funnel to capture the liquid waste 
• There are different funnel attachments for men and women 
• The liquid waste flows through the toilet into the Urine Processor Assembly which is the first 
step in the Water Recovery System  
• For the solid waste the toilet has a plastic bag installed just below the seat 
• With the airflow turned on, the astronaut uses handholds and foot restraints to be positioned 
properly over the toilet 
• When they are finished they tie off the plastic bag and push it down into a storage canister 
connected to the seat 
• Solid waste from the toilet is not recycled 
• When the canister is full it is changed out for an empty, and the full canister is disposed of with 
the rest of the trash on ISS 
• Some supply vehicles that deliver cargo, equipment, food and other supplies to the ISS are 
designed to not return to Earth 
• Instead, after all the supplies have been removed the trash from ISS is loaded into the vehicle, 
it is jettisoned from Station and it burns up in the atmosphere on re-entry

Food on the International Space Station is prepared at Johnson Space Center and shipped to ISS 
before a crew launches. This system is not feasible for a 2+ year mission to Mars or other long

duration space flight. NASA is working with private industry to develop systems that could grow

Big Ideas - ISS Food System 
• All food for the US Segment of ISS is prepared before flight by the Space Food Systems 
Laboratory at Johnson Space Center 
• Russia provides all the food for the cosmonauts in the Russian Segment of ISS 
• Each astronaut has their preferred food packed and labeled for their use 
• Since there is no refrigeration or cooking on ISS, all the food is pre-packaged 
• There is no microwave oven on ISS but there is a warming oven where the user clamps their 
food to heating plates and 20 minutes later the food is hot enough to enjoy 
• Food comes in single servings to avoid leftovers and spoilage 
• Most foods are dehydrated, freeze-dried or packaged in foil pouches similar to military MREs 
(Meals Ready to Eat) 
• Hot and ambient temperature water is available on ISS 
• Labels on dehydrated and freeze-dried foods tell how much water to add and how long to wait 
• When it's ready to eat the user cuts the end off the package and uses a spoon to eat the food 
Big Ideas - Long Duration Flights Might Use Insects 
• It takes 4 days to reach the Moon launching on Artemis 
• It would take from 6 to 9 months to reach Mars going the same speed as Artemis (Artemis is 
not designed to go to Mars) 
• Since Earth and Mars orbit the Sun at different speeds, and are close together only every 26 
months, a trip to Mars will last about 2 years or longer 
• If we use the same food system we have on ISS, a crew of six for 2 years would require about 
20,000 lb of food (10 tons), which is not feasible 
• Experiments on ISS for the last few years have shown we can grow greens (lettuce, kale, 
mustard, Chinese cabbage) as well as hot chile peppers and work is continuing 
• Protein is important for human health but it's not practical to grow cows, pigs or chickens in 
microgravity 
• Insects require much less water and food, are very high in protein and are relatively easy to 
grow in confined spaces 
• Although our Western cultures aren't accustomed to eating insects on a regular basis, many 
people throughout the world use insects as part of their diet 
• NASA and industry are working on ways to grow various plants, insects and fungi in 
microgravity to provide food for long duration space flight 
• We will likely need to process these foods somehow to make them more palatable

The International Space Station is scheduled to be shut down and de-orbited in 2030. NASA is 
working with industry to help them build and operate their own commercial space stations. Once 
operational, NASA will pay these companies to use portions of their stations for its science and 
research needs.

Big Ideas 
• The ISS was first crewed in 2000 and has been continuously occupied since then 
• As time goes by, astronauts on board ISS are spending more time performing maintenance and 
repairs 
• If you drove the same car for 25 years you, too, would be spending more time on repairs and 
maintenance 
• Congress told NASA it would not fund ISS beyond 2030 
• NASA has contracted with SpaceX to develop an ISS de-orbit vehicle that will be able to dock 
with Station, fire its thrusters and push ISS out of orbit 
- Prior to de-orbit all useful resources will be salvaged from Station 
- It will be de-orbited in a controlled manner that will cause it break apart as it enters the 
atmosphere, with some pieces burning up and the remainder falling into the ocean far from 
populated areas 
• NASA is currently supporting the design and development of five commercial space stations

Display: Starliner Pressure Vessel, Vulcan, Dream Chaser – 10 min. 
The Story 
NASA uses commercial launch companies to transport U.S. crew and cargo to ISS. Russia uses 
their own rockets for their crew and cargo. The SpaceX Crew Dragon module is the only U.S. 
commercial crew vehicle operating now. The Boeing Starliner has not yet been certified to carry 
crew or cargo. Two companies currently provide commercial cargo services to ISS: SpaceX 
Cargo Dragon modules (33 ISS missions as of Dec. 2025) and Northrup Grumman Cygnus 
modules (21 ISS missions as of Dec. 2025). Once it is certified, Sierra Space’s Dream Chaser 
may also be used to transport cargo. 
Big Ideas 
• The Boeing Starliner pressure vessel test article (on display here) is the ‘skeleton’ of the 
Starliner 
- It was tested to ensure the design is strong enough to withstand flight loads 
- It is not flight hardware but it is identical to flight hardware 
- It will be covered by panels and other structures to form the outer shell and heat shield of 
Starliner 
• Boeing Starliner holds a crew of up to seven people (only four NASA astronauts when going to 
ISS) 
- It can remain docked to ISS for up to seven months with reusability of up to ten missions 
- Its first successful mission to ISS was launched without a crew in May 2022 
• The second flight was the first crewed mission and carried two NASA astronauts (Suni 
Williams and Butch Wilmore) to ISS in June 2024

Bob Baron was a Huntsville TV meteorologist who decided to develop new tools and computer 
systems to better understand and track severe weather. This exhibit shows how weather 
measurement, analysis and forecasting has evolved over the years.

compared to the Earth (at lower left) 
• The Sun is the driver of all our weather since weather is really the redistribution of heat across 
the Earth 
• On the left wall are early weather instruments, progressing to the advanced instruments used 
today 
• The center aisle focuses on NASA’s Earth observation

Display: Mars Explorers – 15 min. 
The Story 
NASA has sent many missions to Mars, some orbiters and some landers. This exhibit shows 
examples of robotic technologies that we have used to explore the Martian surface. 
Big Ideas 
• Sojourner, 1997 
- First time we sent a rover to Mars 
- Part of the Mars Pathfinder mission 
- After the parachutes opened, Pathfinder became surrounded by airbags (total 19 ft diameter) 
that allowed it to bounce onto Mars after it was released from its parachutes at 300 ft. 
- After landing the airbags deflated, Pathfinder opened up, Sojourner rolled down a ramp 
- Rover was designed to operate for 7 days, lasted 83 days 
- It proved that a rover could be used to explore Mars instead of using only stationary landers 
as we had done up to that point 
- Determined for the first time that Mars was warm and wet at some time in the past 
• Spirit and Opportunity, 2004 
- Identical rovers, landed on opposite sides of Mars 
- Similar to Pathfinder their landers were surrounded by airbags, but larger than those used in 
Pathfinder 
- Designed to operate for 90 days; Spirit lasted 6 yrs, Opportunity 14 yrs 
- Eventually Spirit broke through a crust and got stuck in very soft sand 
- Opportunity was caught in a global-scale dust storm and the very cold Martian nights 
drained its batteries 
- They both discovered evidence that the planet once hosted running water before becoming a 
frozen desert

- Opportunity found round conglomerates that had formed in running water during a warmer 
past (see the 'blueberries' display on the wall) 
- Also discovered that all the Martian dust is somewhat magnetic and made of iron oxide 
(similar to rust) 
• Curiosity, 2012  (Perseverance, not shown, landed in 2021 and is very similar to Curiosity) 
- Curiosity and Perseverance were five times heavier than Spirit and Opportunity, too heavy 
for air bags 
- Retro rockets strong enough to land them on the surface would have created craters that the 
rovers might get stuck in 
- Curiosity was the first time a 'sky crane' was used to land a rover (see video on the wall) 
- Nine years later Perseverance also used a sky crane 
- Both Curiosity and Perseverance were required to operate for at least one Mars year, which 
is 687 Earth days or 1.9 Earth years 
- As of 2026, Curiosity has been on Mars 14 Earth years and Perseverance has been there 5 
years 
- Curiosity discovered that its landing site, Gale Crater, hosted a lake billions of years ago, as 
well as an environment that could have supported microbial life 
- Perseverance aims to find out if there are signs of past microbial life on Mars 
- Instruments on Perseverance are similar but upgraded versions of those on Curiosity 
- They both have a laser that can vaporize a spot on a rock up to 23 ft away then analyze the 
spectra of the resulting plasma to determine the makeup of that rock 
- If scientists want to investigate the rock further, Curiosity will roll closer then drill into the 
rock and use its instruments to analyze it 
- Perseverance can drill and analyze but it can also detect the presence of organic compounds 
in rocks and regolith either up close or from a distance 
- Perseverance can store rock and soil samples in tubes on board the rover 
- Originally the plan was to eventually send them back to Earth for analysis, but this program 
has been cancelled so there are currently no plans to return the samples to Earth 
• Ingenuity Helicopter, 2021 
- Arrived with Perseverance, first aircraft to achieve powered controlled flight on another 
planet 
- Successfully flew in the extremely thin Martian atmosphere which is only about 1% as dense 
as Earth 
- High risk experiment, planned to fly just 5 times over 30 days to prove its ability to fly on 
Mars 
- It was so successful it was used as an aerial scout for scientists and rover drivers to access 
terrain that was difficult to reach and send back pictures for possible investigations 
- On 72nd flight it landed at an angle and broke the propellers 
- Ended up flying 72 times over 1005 days, or 2 3/4 years 
• Insight, 2018-2022 (4 years) 
- It's a lander, not a rover 
- Curiosity and Perseverance were designed to study the geology of Mars 
- Insight was designed to study the interior structure of Mars 
- Two solar panels provided power until they were covered with dust

Display: Space Suits – 5 min. 
The Story 
Space suits are necessary to keep astronauts alive. The suits have evolved over time to meet the 
changing needs of the space program and as technology has improved. 
Big Ideas 
• Mercury: Mercury suits were designed to protect astronauts inside the spacecraft in case of a 
pressure leak 
- They were not designed to be worn outside the vehicle because NASA wasn't ready for 
spacewalks yet 
- The shiny outer layer of the suit was made of aluminized nylon and the inner pressure layer 
was made of nylon fabric with a rubber coating 
• Gemini: Gemini suits were similar to Mercury suits but the outer aluminized nylon was 
replaced by Nomex 
- This allowed the suits to be used both inside the vehicle and outside for spacewalks 
• Apollo: Apollo suits were dramatically different than those used in Mercury and Gemini 
because they had to keep the astronauts safe on the Moon, as well as while they were inside the 
vehicle during launch and entry (i.e. atmospheric re-entry and landing) 
- The suits had to provide a breathable environment on the Moon as well as protect the 
astronauts from jagged rocks, Moon dust, radiation, micrometeoroids and temperatures as 
high as 250 F while being flexible enough for them to bend down and pick up rocks 
- One company who helped make these suits, International Latex, got the contract in part 
because they were experienced in making garments that were durable yet flexible – they 
made Playtex bras and undergarments 
- The layer closest to the skin included liquid-filled tubing that was temperature-controlled to 
keep the astronaut comfortable 
- The other layers provided thermal insulation, maintained atmospheric pressure and protected 
against abrasion 
- A backpack provided life support, thermal control and communications 
• Space Shuttle Ejection Escape Suit: Worn by astronauts during the first four Shuttle missions 
while NASA was still testing the safety of the reusable spacecraft 
- It provided oxygen and protection in case of depressurization or ejection from the Shuttle, as 
well as protection from high-altitude cold temperatures 
- The first four shuttle flights were equipped with specially adapted SR-71 ejection seats for 
the two crew members in case of emergency 
- Starting with the fifth Shuttle flight the ejection seats were no longer used

Big Ideas, Additional Info 
• Hubble Space Telescope 
- Serviced five times by Space Shuttle, each time installing upgraded instruments and cameras 
(servicing missions 1993, 1997, 1999, 2002, 2009) 
- Currently in a 95-minute circular obit around Earth about 330 miles in altitude 
- Can operate with only one working gyroscope but moves much slower between targets 
- The second gyroscope is not being used, it's a backup for when the primary gyroscope fails 
• Compton Gamma Ray Observatory 
- Collected data on some of the most violent physical processes in the Universe, characterized 
by their extremely high energies 
- In June 2000, although it was still operating, NASA decided to intentionally de-orbit 
Compton due to the loss of one of its three gyroscopes 
- After Skylab's 1979 uncontrolled re-entry NASA's policy is to de-orbit satellites before they 
lose control of them 
- Loss of a second gyroscope would have made it very difficult to control the de-orbit 
- Compton broke apart as it fell through the atmosphere and those items that didn’t burn up 
fell into the ocean 
• Chandra X-Ray Observatory 
- Observes objects such as black holes, quasars, and high-temperature gases throughout the x-
ray portion of the electromagnetic spectrum 
- During each 64-hour orbit of the Earth, Chandra travels about one-third of the way to the 
Moon (orbit is 6,000 x 86,000 miles; Moon is 240,000 miles away) 
- Chandra’s orbit takes it over 250 times higher than the Hubble Space Telescope 
- Chandra uses two sets of four nested hollow cylindrical glass mirrors, stacked one in front of 
the other, to focus incoming X-rays on science instruments about 30 ft away 
- The mirror is made of Zerodur, a type of glass with an extremely low coefficient of thermal 
expansion, that is, it's shape doesn't change much at all when the temperature changes 
• Spitzer Space Telescope 
- Spitzer used its 2.8 ft diameter beryllium mirror to capture light in infrared at wavelengths 
from near to far infrared (3 to 160 microns) 
- It revealed objects that previously couldn't be studied such as stellar nurseries, the centers of 
galaxies, newly forming planetary systems, failed stars (brown dwarfs), extrasolar planets, 
giant molecular clouds, and organic molecules that may hold the secret to life on other 
planets 
- When it ran out of cryogens to cool its instruments after 6 years (the requirement was 2 1/2 
years), it continued to operate using non-cooled instruments 
- It reached end-of-life after 17 years and continues in its original orbit, trailing in Earth’s 
orbit and gradually getting farther away

Display: ISS Water Recovery and Food 
Big Ideas, Additional Info - Water Recycling 
• Steps in the water recovery process 
- Distillation: Urine is heated to create a vapor, leaving behind contaminants and creating 
partially distilled water 
- Filtration Beds: Uses a series of filters with varying pore sizes to remove particulate matter, 
bacteria, and other impurities; activated carbon filters remove organic compounds and odors 
- Ion Exchange Resins: Removes dissolved ions, like chlorides and sulfates 
- Catalytic Oxidation Reactor: Uses a catalyst and heat to break down remaining organic 
contaminants into harmless substances 
- Iodine: Added to prevent microbial growth during storage

Not currently in the Atrium: James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) 
(The JWST model is in the Lunar Lounge in the Planetarium Lobby) 
The Story 
The James Webb Space Telescope is designed to see objects in space that are so far away that 
only their infrared light reaches us. 
Big Ideas 
• Webb operates about a million miles from Earth, orbiting a gravitationally stable point in space 
called L2 (the 2nd Lagrange Point) 
• JWST does not have a telescope tube like normal telescopes because the tube is used to block 
stray light and JWST uses its sunshield for this purpose 
- The sunshield is about the size of a tennis court (sunshield 70x47 ft; tennis court 78x36 ft) 
- Webb sunshield has 5 layers but only requires 4 layers (our Webb model is 1/20 scale and 
has only 3 layers of sunshield) 
- The layers are made of Kapton (similar to Mylar) – each layer is thinner than a human hair 
- The primary mirror always faces deep space to maintain its -388°F operating temperature 
- The bottom of Webb always faces the Sun and Earth and operates at +185°F 
- The sunshield blocks 500 degrees of heat 
• Primary mirror is 21 ft across, made up of 18 mirror segments that can be individually 
controlled, aligned, and focused to act as one big mirror 
- Beryllium mirror segments are about 4 ft across, 2 inch thick 
- Back of segments were machined into honeycomb shape, 92% of material was removed, 
lightweight yet strong (before: 550 lb, after: 46 lb) 
- Like all materials, beryllium changes shape when it gets cold but not as much as other 
materials 
- Mirror segments had to be machined and polished exactly wrong at room temperature so 
they would deform into exactly the right shape when they reached their -388°F operating 
temperature 
- Segments are coated with gold because that’s the best reflector of infrared light 
- Each segment is coated with 1/10 oz of gold (600 atoms thick, 1/1000 thickness of human 
hair), totaling about half the weight of a man’s wedding ring 
- The gold on all 18 segments together weighs 1.7 oz (48 g) – about the weight of a golf ball 
(NOT the size of a golf ball) 
• There are four Science Instruments that look at different parts of the infrared spectrum to 
observe different things (NIRCam, MIRI, NIRSpec, NIRISS) 
- Some are cameras and some are spectrographs, which analyze the light of an object to 
determine its temperature, mass, and chemical composition 
- There is also a coronagraph that blocks out the light of a bright object like a star so you can 
see nearby faint objects like exoplanets
