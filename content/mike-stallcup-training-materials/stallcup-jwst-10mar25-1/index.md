James E. Webb was NASA Administrator from February 1961 to October 1968. He believed that NASA had to strike a balance between human space flight and science because such a combination would serve as a catalyst for strengthening the nation's universities and aerospace industry. By the time he retired in early 1969 NASA had launched 75 space science missions, including development of robotic spacecraft, which explored the lunar environment so that astronauts could do so later, and sent scientific probes to Mars and Venus.
Concept of JWST started in 1996 [25 years before launch]; construction started in 2004 [17 years before launch]; launch Dec. 2021
Took a long time to develop technologies for lightweight segmented mirrors with this level of performance that met cost and schedule requirements. It took 11 years to mature mirror technology from Tech Readiness Level [TRL] 3 to 6.
Required new material [O-30 Beryllium, less cryo-distortion, better surface finish], improved polishing & measuring techniques, segmented mirror provides better cryo-stability
Cost to build, launch and commission the observatory was $8.8B with five years of operations at $860M for a total lifecycle cost of $9.66B. ESA contributed €700 million  and CSA [Canada] contributed CA$200 million.

{{< slide n="4" img="slides/slide-004.webp" >}}
What does JWST hope to accomplish?
We’re going to search for the first galaxies formed shortly after the Big Bang, which occurred about 13.8 billion years ago. [13.79±0.02 billion years ago]. In fact, the first Webb image included a galaxy formed only 330 million years after the Big Bang [13.6 billion years ago]
We want to determine how galaxies evolved from their formation until now. Scientists want to study how matter is currently organized and how that organization has changed through time.
We will observe the formation of stars from their first stages to full-blown planetary systems to help answer questions like: How do clouds of gas and dust collapse to form stars? Why do most stars form in groups? How do planetary systems form?
We will measure the physical and chemical properties of planetary systems, and investigate the potential for life in those systems. Webb can analyze some of the components in the atmosphere of planets to look for chemicals and gases that could be favorable for life.

The Webb Telescope is designed to capture infrared light. With it we have been able to see things we’ve never seen before.
But infrared light is heat, so the telescope has to be very cold to capture that heat from distant galaxies. That’s why it's in an orbit around the second Lagrange point, known as L2, about a million miles from Earth [930,000 mi; 1.5 M km]. < Point to L2> 
For reference, the Moon is about 239,000 miles from Earth, so L2 is about 4 times as far from Earth as the Moon is. 
And, in case you were wondering, Mars averages about 142 million miles from Earth, or about 600 times as far from Earth as the Moon is. [It ranges from 35 to 250 M mi, 150 to 1000 times the distance from Earth to our Moon.]
L2 is a place in space that, due to the gravity of the Sun and the Earth, anything you put there will stay there with only occasional rocket firings.
<Advance to the next slide to trigger the animation movie>
The bottom of Webb is the hot side and it always faces the Sun and the Earth; the top side with the mirror always faces the cold of deep space.
In operation the bottom is at +185 F [85 C, 358 K] and water boils at 212 F [100 C] so that’s only 27 degrees below boiling. The top is at -388 F [-233 C, 39 K], just 71 degrees above Absolute Zero [-459 F, -273 C, 0 K].
Because of this orbit Webb will always be in view from Earth so we will have very good communications. It won’t be going in and out of Earth’s shadow like the Hubble Space Telescope does, which could cause thermal issues on Webb. [HST orbits @ 325 mi.]
We will use NASA’s Deep Space Network to upload a week’s worth of commands at once with updates every day. BTW, radio signals take about 5 seconds to travel from L2 to Earth.
And the people Webb is talking to are at the Space Telescope Science Institute [STSci] in Baltimore, Maryland who manage Webb's science operations.
This Institute also performs this function for the Hubble Space Telescope and the upcoming Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope that's scheduled to launch in 2026.

We’ll start with the sunshield.
The sunshield keeps the heat from the Sun and the Earth from getting to the primary mirror. 
It measures about 70 ft x 47 ft [21.2 m x 14.2 m], about the size of a tennis court [78x36 ft].
It’s made of 5 layers of Kapton, a material similar to Mylar emergency blankets or Mylar balloons or the sun reflector you might use on the dashboard of your car.
Each layer is thinner than a human hair but very strong. 
[L1 & L2=0.002 in (1/2 hair), L3 to L5=0.001 in. (1/4 hair). Hair/paper is 0.004 inch (0.1 mm) thick.]
There are reinforcing lines every 6 feet. It functions similar to ripstop nylon, that is, if we get a tear in one of the layers it will run until it hits one of those reinforcing lines.
All layers are coated with aluminum and the two hottest sun-facing layers also have silicon added to their coating to protect from the Sun’s UV radiation. This pink coating also helps them give off any heat they absorb.
The top layer is mostly for margin against imperfections, micro-meteoroid holes, etc.
Micro-meteoroids are generally so small they disintegrate when they hit the first layer.
The sunshield blocks over 500 degrees of heat to keep the primary mirror cold, so let’s talk about that mirror…

{{< slide n="8" img="slides/slide-008.webp" >}}
The primary mirror has to be kept very cold, so it faces deep space.
Whereas the Hubble Space Telescope has a tube to block stray light, Webb has a sunshield instead.
The picture at bottom center gives you an idea of the relative sizes of Webb and Hubble.
The picture at top center shows the sizes of the primary mirrors with respect to a person.
Webb’s primary mirror is 21 ft [6.5 m] across and is made of 18 individual mirror segments.
The Hubble primary mirror is one piece 8 ft [2.4 m] across, made of a special kind of glass called ULE.
The Webb mirror weighs about 30% less than Hubble’s mirror but has 6 times the collecting area.
The operating temperature of the Webb mirror is -360 to -400 F [-220 to -240 C; 54 to 34 K].
These very cold temperatures are known as ‘cryogenic temperatures.’
Webb’s 18 mirror segments are made of a metal called ‘beryllium’ and they are individually adjustable.
The primary mirror has 2 side wings that contain the 3 mirrors on the left and the 3 mirrors on the right, as you can see in the upper left photo.
These wings were folded back behind the primary mirror before launch.
It had to be designed this way so Webb could fit into the rocket that launched it.
The upper right photo shows Webb before it launched, all folded up and ready to go into the nosecone of the rocket.
On orbit the faces of the 18 mirror segments need to line up with each other to a fraction of a wavelength of light, that is, to about 50 nm or 2 millionths inch.  [1 nm = 0.04 millionths inch, 1 billionth meter].
To give you an idea of how small this is, imagine that the Webb primary mirror were the size of the United States (the lower 48 states, that is).
If the whole mirror were that big, then each segment would be about the size of Texas.
And the faces of the mirror segments would need to line up with each other within 1.5 inches.
This mirror alignment is fine-tuned once every 10 to 14 days.
Webb can capture as little light as 1 photon per second, requiring very long exposures, and its extremely stable pointing allows for exposures of up to 10 days.

{{< slide n="9" img="slides/slide-009.webp" >}}
Beryllium metal is light, stiff, and very stable at low temperatures.
Each mirror segment is about 4 ft tall [1.3 m; 4’3”] and 2 inches thick [50 mm].
The upper left photo shows the back side of one of the mirrors. The honeycomb pattern is the result of removing un-needed material while still providing the necessary strength.
This started out as a solid beryllium blank weighing 550 lb [250 kg]. After removing all the unnecessary material it weighed 46 lb [21 kg], that is, 92% of the material was removed.
Each mirror segment can be moved in all 6 directions, and its Radius of Curvature can be slightly adjusted to change its focus.
The upper right photo shows the mechanisms on the back of a mirror segment that makes this possible.
The photo at the lower left shows a technician monitoring the grinding of one of the mirror segments.
After grinding, the face of the segments were polished to an average roughness of 1/5000 the thickness of a human hair [20 nm, 0.8 millionths inch; hair = 100,000 nm].
Like all materials, beryllium changes shape when it gets cold. This could cause a problem because the mirrors are ground and polished at room temperature, but they operate at cryogenic temperatures.
As a result, to get a perfect mirror at cryogenic temperatures we must grind it exactly wrong at room temperature so it will deform to exactly the right shape when it gets cold.
The center photo shows 6 segments ready to be tested at cryogenic temperature in the X-Ray Cryogenic Facility at NASA/Marshall Space Flight Center about 10 miles from here. Every mirror segment went through that same testing before it was installed on the telescope.
The mirrors are coated with pure gold because gold is the best material to reflect infrared light.
The segments are then topped with a very thin coating of glass [SiO2] to protect it from handling scratches, and dust which could cause scratches.
Each segment is coated with less than 1/10 oz of gold [3g], about half the weight of a man’s wedding ring.
This gold coating is only 600 atoms thick, and is about 1/1000 the thickness of a human hair [100 nm, 4 millionths inch].
The gold on all 18 segments together weighs about 1.7 oz [48 g] – about the weight of a golf ball (not the size of a golf ball)…

{{< slide n="13" img="slides/slide-013.webp" >}}
Gyroscopes are part of the control system that helps point an observatory as well as keeping it stable enough to take long exposure pictures of distant objects.
The gyroscopes on Webb are different than the gyroscopes used on Hubble.
On Hubble they are made of a small flywheel spinning very fast which uses gas bearings, not ball bearings.
Four of Hubble's 6 gyroscopes have failed, so it's currently operating with only one gyroscope, holding the other one in reserve.
Hubble can still function but it has limitations on where it can point and how fast it can move, which is not bad for an orbiting observatory that is 35 years old.
Webb uses a different kind of gyroscope made of a fused quartz glass hemisphere mounted on a thick stem, so it looks sort of like a wine glass.
They don’t spin like other gyroscopes. Instead, they vibrate and their motion is constantly measured.
Since they have no moving parts they are extremely reliable and highly accurate. Their expected life is about 10 million hours, which is over 1100 years.
There are 6 of these on Webb but it only needs 2 to function, which is good because we don’t have any way to replace them as Webb orbits L2 about a million miles from Earth…
 
—— Extra Info ——
In 2009 the Space Shuttle performed the last of five Hubble servicing missions. Astronauts replaced all six of the gyroscopes with new ones that used the latest technology at the time.
Webb gyroscopes are 'Hemispherical Resonator Gyroscope'.

The sunshield has five layers. We can actually function with four layers, but by having five layers we can stand to have some minor damage to some of the layers.
Each layer of the sunshield has reinforcing lines every 6 feet so it acts like rip-stop nylon.
At L2 we don’t have near as much orbital debris as we have in orbit around the Earth. At L2 it’s mostly just dust particles that disintegrate when they hit the sunshield. If they do penetrate, they don’t go through more than one layer.
When engineers were designing the Webb mirror they knew there would be micrometeoroids hitting it. The mirror surface was designed to be better than it really needs to be, so even after years of small impacts it will still function adequately.

As of September 2023 Webb had sustained 51 impacts at an average rate of 2.6 per month.
To minimize further impact they now allow only a few observations with the mirror pointed in the direction of travel…

The requirement for the life of the observatory is that, once it’s in orbit around L2, it must last at least 5 years with a goal of 10 years.
The Chandra X-Ray Observatory that’s currently orbiting the Earth was designed to last 5 years, but it’s still functioning after 26 years.
The Hubble Space Telescope, which is also orbiting Earth, was designed to last 5 years, but it’s still functioning after 35 years, although it has been serviced by the Space Shuttle 5 times.
Webb doesn’t have anything that gets used up except propellant. There are no on-board consumable cryogenic coolants as some observatories have had in the past.
Earlier I mentioned that the MIRI instrument on Webb has a cryo-cooler because it operates so much colder than the other instruments. This cryo-cooler is like a super-powerful refrigerator that recycles its coolant.
In terms of station keeping, Webb’s orbit around L2 takes about 6 months. As I explained earlier, because of the gravity of the Sun and Earth, anything you put into orbit around L2 doesn’t require many thruster firings to stay in orbit.
Solar radiation pressure pushes on the sunshield, but reaction wheels counteract this. Eventually, the accumulated momentum in the reaction wheels need to be dumped by firing thrusters. Webb has 8 pairs of 1 pound hydrazine thrusters for this purpose.
The current plan is for Webb’s on-board thrusters to fire about every 21 days to keep it in orbit.
Since the rocket that launched Webb did such a good job of delivering it right on target, Webb didn’t need to make as many trajectory-correction firings as expected, so it ended up with more propellant on-board than expected when it finally went into orbit around L2.
As a result, engineers believe Webb has enough propellant on-board to last at least 20 years.

Electromagnetic radiation is all around us. Some of it we recognize as light, but there’s a lot we can’t see.
Let’s start on the left with Gamma radiation: You won’t deal with this very much unless you’re a physicist or you work at a nuclear power plant.
Next we have X-ray: This is handy when you have a broken bone, but it is also given off by extremely hot objects in space so some astronomers are very interested in it. Our atmosphere absorbs almost all the X-rays coming from space so we’re safe from those. The Chandra X-ray Observatory is currently in orbit around the Earth and is similar to Hubble but it captures images in X-ray.
Then we have Ultraviolet (also known as UV): Our atmosphere absorbs most of this but enough gets through to cause a sunburn if you’re exposed too long. Hot objects in space give off UV radiation so that’s another way for astronomers to study things in space, but UV doesn’t penetrate interstellar gas and dust very well.
Followed by Visible: This is the light we can see with our eyes. And this is mostly what Hubble captures.
After that is Infrared: Our atmosphere absorbs much of this, but some of it from the sun passes through and we feel it as heat. Everything gives off some level of infrared radiation, depending on how hot it is. Astronomers can learn a lot about things in space by looking at their infrared radiation. Also, infrared light passes through gas and dust quite well. This is what Webb will capture. Spitzer was a space telescope that also captured infrared and gave us some amazing images, but it stopped working in 2020. By the way, Webb’s primary mirror has 50 times the collecting area that Spitzer had.
Next to last is Microwave: The same microwave radiation that heats your lunch can be observed by astronomers looking into space to test theories about the birth and evolution of the Universe. Our atmosphere absorbs part of the microwaves coming from space but some others can get through.
And finally Radio: The Radio frequency band is large enough to allow us to hear our favorite songs in the car and, at different frequencies, allow radio astronomers to detect radio signals from space. Ground based radio telescopes work quite well but require arrays of huge dish antennas on the ground.
Since our atmosphere absorbs many of the frequencies that astronomers want to use to observe things in space, we use space-based-telescope-satellites to get above our atmosphere. Chandra, Hubble, Spitzer and Webb are only the most recent examples…

Now let’s talk about Redshift.
The universe is expanding and everything is moving away from everything else.
A good analogy for the expansion of the universe is raisin bread. Imagine you’re making a loaf of raisin bread and you’ve just mixed up your dough. The next step is to let it rise. As the yeast causes the bread to rise and the dough to expand, the raisins in the dough will all move away from each other. This is what’s happening with everything in the universe. Everything is moving away from everything else.
And when things move away fast enough the light waves stretch out and the stretching causes the light waves to turn from white to red.
This is called a ‘redshift’ and is similar to the Doppler effect in sound which lowers the pitch of a sound that’s moving away from you quickly because the sound waves stretch out.
There comes a point where the light waves stretch out so much that they change from red to infrared.
Things that have been moving away from us for a long time are the farthest away, so all their light is in the infrared range and they are very faint. 
The purpose of Webb is to see very old things that are very far away from us in space. To see these things you need a big light bucket, that is, a large mirror that collects infrared light. And that’s what the Webb telescope has…

These images illustrate how technology has sped up the imaging process.
Although these are different areas of the sky, they are both deep field images. That is, images taken over a long period of time of an apparently dark part of the sky.
The image on the left was taken with visible light by Hubble in 2004, and it took 278 hours to capture 800 individual images to create this one mosaic image.
The image on the right was taken with infrared light by Webb in July 2022. Webb’s mirror, which gathers 6 times more light than Hubble, combined with advancements in imaging technology, produced the image on the right in 12.5 hours.
To give you an idea of what you’re looking at in the Webb image on the right, hold out your arm and point your finger.

{{< slide n="20" img="slides/slide-020.webp" >}}
In many images we see sparkling stars. If you were to see stars from a spaceship, they wouldn’t have these ‘light rays’. They would just be points of light. The light rays appear when we look through a telescope. 
Incoming light waves interact with mirror edges and struts that hold the secondary mirror, as shown in the upper right picture. These surfaces bend the light to create these ‘spikes’. This bending of light is called ‘diffraction.’
This only happens on bright point sources of light, like stars, not fuzzy things like galaxies.
All images containing bright stars taken through reflecting telescopes will have these spikes.
On images from Webb you’ll see 6 spikes, on Hubble’s images you’ll see only 4 spikes.
This is because Webb’s diffracted light comes from the primary mirror’s edges and the 3 struts holding the secondary mirror, which are highlighted in red in the upper right picture.
Hubble has no mirror edges to diffract the light and it has 4 struts holding the secondary mirror…

{{< slide n="21" img="slides/slide-021.webp" >}}
Here we have images of the Tarantula Nebula taken by two different instruments on Webb.
These images illustrate how you can see different things when you use instruments that capture different frequencies of infrared light.
For a long time the Tarantula Nebula has been a favorite with astronomers who study star formation.
The NIRCam image (on the left) shows thousands of never-before-seen young stars that were previously shrouded in cosmic dust. The most active region sparkles with massive young stars, appearing pale blue.
The MIRI image (on the right), which is designed to show cooler gas and dust instead of hot stars, shows much cooler and younger protostars growing from that dust.
Scientists are interested in gas and dust because that's the raw material that stars are formed from.
At this point, let me talk a little about the colors you see in these and other images.
The raw images coming from the Webb, Hubble and Chandra telescopes are actually just black and white pictures, but along with them there is other information that comes from filters on the telescopes.
NASA has specialists who use that other information to choose colors to emphasize different things, depending on what scientists are interested in seeing.
In Webb images the colors correspond to different frequencies of infrared light. In images from Chandra X-ray Observatory, colors correspond to different energies of X-rays. In Hubble’s visible light images the colors correspond to different elements. For instance, nitrogen might be red, hydrogen could be green, and oxygen could be blue. It all depends on what the scientists are looking for…

{{< slide n="22" img="slides/slide-022.webp" >}}
The galaxy cluster G165 has been imaged by Hubble several times, but this image from Webb shows detail that hasn't been seen before.
When galaxies cluster like this, their combined mass warps the fabric of space to form a ‘gravitational lens’ that distorts and magnifies the light from background objects.
The orange arced lines you see in this image are very distant ‘lensed galaxies’ located behind the galaxy clusters we see.
Not only do gravity lenses make distant galaxies look larger, but they also make them brighter so it’s easier to see the very distant faint galaxies that formed soon after the Big Bang...
   
————- Extra Info —————-
High-gravity objects, such as galaxy clusters, cause lensed galaxies to appear at multiple distorted locations and they are magnified in size and brightness beyond what they would appear without the lensing.
Multiple images of a lensed galaxy can be identified by comparing characteristic features in each image such as: shapes and locations of objects (morphology) including substructures, luminance or brightness, color, wavelengths of non-visible light, spectral energies, and levels of redshift. Images of the same galaxy from other observatories (such as HST, Spitzer, ground-based, etc.) can be used to enhance and corroborate these analyses…

{{< slide n="23" img="slides/slide-023.webp" >}}
The close-up of G165 on the right shows three versions of the same lensed galaxy.
Note that the lensed galaxy is not the three dashed circles but rather the fuzzy bright areas next to the dashed circles.
Astronomers have been studying this galaxy with Hubble and Webb for quite a while.
However, in this recent image from Webb they noticed three new, tiny, bright dots that are inside the dashed circles.
It turns out these are three views of a supernova that had recently occurred, but to get three images the light had to travel along three different paths.
Since each path had a different length, and light always travels at the same speed, the supernova was captured in this image at three different times during its explosion.
By analyzing these three views of the supernova, astronomers were able to better understand the universe's expansion rate, that seems to change depending on how it's measured.
This difference in calculated expansion rates is known as the 'Hubble Tension' and scientists are actively working to resolve it.
And while we're talking about little dots in Webb images, recent research on small, very red galaxies from the early universe (only 600 million years after the Big Bang) suggests that many of them contain young black holes along with stars instead of just a huge number of stars.
Early reports that Webb had 'broken cosmology' were because the light from these early galaxies was much brighter than expected for their age, which would mean they had many more stars than our theories would predict.
Now astronomers believe the spinning disks of very hot and very bright gas and dust around the growing black holes, known as accretion disks, are giving off that extra light. So Webb has NOT actually broken cosmology...
  
  ———— Extra info ————
The 'Hubble Tension' is the fact that the current rate of the expansion of the universe is faster than what astronomers expect it to be, based on the universe's initial conditions and our present understanding of the universe’s evolution.
Different analysis techniques have produced different values for the 'Hubble Constant' which is related to the speed of the universe's expansion.
The analysis of this new supernova has provided more information related to this difference.
Astronomers are still working on it...

{{< slide n="25" img="slides/slide-025.webp" >}}
Shifting from new stars to old stars, this image of the star WR124 is a rarely-seen phenomena, a star rapidly on its way to being a supernova. Many stars skip this phase before they become a supernova.
This one has not exploded yet. Instead, it’s in the process of casting off its outer layers, resulting in this characteristic halo of gas and dust.
Originally it was 40 times bigger than our Sun but it has already shed 1/4 of its mass.
As the ejected gas moves away from the star and cools, cosmic dust forms and glows, which we see as red in this image.
Astronomers study cosmic dust because it can shelter new stars as they form, it can gather together to help form planets, and it can help molecules form and clump together which is how scientists think the building blocks of life on Earth were formed...
   
  ———— Extra info ————
A supernova occurs when a star that is more than 5 times the size of our Sun runs out of fuel and its gravity causes it to suddenly collapse in less than a minute. This results in a massive shockwave that causes the outer part of the star to explode, leaving behind a very dense core and an expanding cloud of hot gas called a nebula. If the sun is more than 10 times the size of our sun, the remaining core could become a black hole. This image is NOT a nebula after a supernova, it’s a star giving off material BEFORE it becomes a supernova.

{{< slide n="27" img="slides/slide-027.webp" >}}
The Crab Nebula, the result of a bright supernova explosion seen by Chinese and other astronomers in the year 1054, is 6500 light-years from Earth, which is relatively close.
Scientists study it to understand the conditions, behavior, and after-effects of supernovae.
At the center of the Crab Nebula is a special kind of neutron star called a pulsar, a super-dense star produced by the supernova, that rotates at about 30 times per second.
A high-speed wind of matter and anti-matter particles is thrown off and plows into the surrounding nebula, creating a shock wave that forms the expanding rings.
Jets from the poles of the pulsar spew X-ray-emitting matter and antimatter particles in a direction perpendicular to the rings.
Scientists are using this image, which combines X-ray data from Chandra and infrared data from Webb, to help understand what type of explosion produced the Crab Nebula...

{{< slide n="28" img="slides/slide-028.webp" >}}
When astronomers first saw this image they thought there was something wrong with their instruments because it looks very much like a classic optical aberration called a 'diffraction pattern.'
When they looked closely at the data it turned out that these are actually concentric shells of dust formed as two stars orbit each other.
As the stars swing past one another (within the central white dot), the stellar winds from each star slam together, the material compresses, and carbon-rich dust forms.
Carbon compounds are important because they are necessary for the formation of rocky planets and solar systems like ours.
The even spacing between the shells indicates that dust formation events are occurring like clockwork, once every eight years.
To get a feel for how large this is, the distance from the center to the outer-most shell is about 70,000 times the distance from Earth to the Sun. There are more shells farther out but they are so faint we can't see them...

{{< slide n="31" img="slides/slide-031.webp" >}}
This shimmering cosmic curtain shows interstellar gas and dust that has been heated by the explosion of a long-ago supernova.
This heated gas and dust, known as the interstellar medium, can't be seen in visible light but it glows with infrared light in this image as a 'thermal light echo'.
A light echo is a phenomena in space where light from something that happened in the past is illuminating something in the present.
This happens because things are so far apart in space that it takes a long time for light to travel between different regions.
In this image Webb has captured that infrared glow, revealing fine details that look like wood grain.
Astronomers didn't know that the interstellar medium had structures on this small of a scale, let alone that it was sheet-like because they had never been able to look inside them before.
Webb actually took three images of this area at different times to see how the structure changed over time.
These observations are allowing scientists to map the true 3D structure of this interstellar medium for the very first time...
   
————- Extra Info —————-
They suspect that every dense, dusty region they see in space (and most of the ones they don’t see) would look like this on the inside, but they have never been able to look inside them before.
