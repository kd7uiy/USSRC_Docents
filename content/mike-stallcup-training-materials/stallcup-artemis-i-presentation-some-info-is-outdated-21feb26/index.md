{{< slide n="4" img="slides/slide-004.webp" >}}
In the early 2000s the decision was made to phase out the Space Shuttle program and return to NASA’s roots – deep space exploration.
The Shuttle program ended in 2011 after 30 years, but we evolved the well-understood Shuttle elements (the engines, external tank, and solid rocket boosters) into the Space Launch System (called SLS), which became part of the Artemis program.
The Orion crew module, also part of the Artemis program, is similar to the Apollo command module, but it uses the latest technology. It even has it’s own internal Wi-Fi network.
One of the reasons the Shuttle was retired was because we could buy crew and cargo missions to International Space Station from commercial suppliers like SpaceX, Boeing, and eventually Sierra Space.
The Artemis program, of which SLS and Orion are the first pieces, will go to the Moon and beyond.
Eventually the Artemis program will include other elements, such as the Gateway which is a mini space station that will be in orbit around the Moon.
Now let’s talk about the goals of the Artemis program…
{{< /slide >}}

{{< slide n="5" img="slides/slide-005.webp" >}}
This is an artist’s concept of a lunar base camp.
The Artemis program goals are to:
To send people back to the Moon
Establish a sustainable presence on and around the Moon
Explore more of the lunar surface than ever before
Develop lunar resources such as energy, minerals, water, etc.
Collaborate with commercial and international partners 
And use what we learn on and around the Moon to send astronauts to Mars

{{< slide n="6" img="slides/slide-006.webp" >}}
This slide compares several vehicles and their payload capacity to Low Earth Orbit, commonly referred to as LEO.
I’ve included the Statue of Liberty to give you a feeling for the size of these vehicles.
At launch the Space Launch System rocket, which we call the SLS, will produce 13% more thrust than the Space Shuttle and 15% more than the Saturn V during liftoff and ascent. [The Saturn V upper stage is more powerful than the ICPS on SLS.]
Note that the Shuttle was designed to only go to low Earth orbit, like SpaceX's Falcon 9, not to go to the Moon like the others.
In its first configuration, shown here, the SLS is designed to carry about 95 metric tons to LEO.
By the way, I’ll be speaking in metric tons, which is equal to 2204 lb. In the U.S. we usually talk about ‘tons’, also known as ‘short tons’, which are equal to 2000 lb. Since these spacecraft are rated in metric tons that’s what I’ll be using.
Eventually, the plan is to upgrade SLS to carry 130 metric tons.
Saturn V could carry about 141 metric tons to LEO.
SpaceX is still evolving the Starship and its stated payload capacity varies widely.

{{< slide n="7" img="slides/slide-007.webp" >}}
Here we have an exploded view of the SLS (which we hope we’ll never see in real life!)
When we were trying to figure out what the SLS should look like, we looked at Saturn and Apollo and realized they did a lot of things right.
For one thing, they had the astronauts in a crew module at the top of the rocket, away from all the fire and smoke at the other end where things could go wrong.
By putting the Orion crew module at the top of SLS, we also avoid the issue of foam shedding. In the Shuttle program we had a problem with pieces of insulating foam coming off the External Tank and striking the Orbiter which was mounted on the side of the External Tank. With Orion mounted on the top this won’t be an issue.
The big orange structure in the middle is the Core Stage, <Point to Core Stage> which is similar to the external tank from the Shuttle program, but the SLS internal hydrogen and oxygen tanks are bigger. We are still using foam insulation on Core Stage to keep the liquid hydrogen at -423 degrees F and the liquid oxygen at -297 F which helps prevent a buildup of ice on the outside of the tank when it’s sitting on the launch pad in very humid Florida.  [Absolute zero = -460 F]
These tanks hold the fuel for the four RS-25 engines mounted on the aft end of the Core Stage. <Point to Engines> These are the same engines that were used on the Shuttle, which were designed to be re-used 30 times. At the end of the Shuttle program there were 16 engines left over, so we have enough engines for the first four flights of Artemis. The contractor that built these, Aerojet Rocketdyne, is now building upgraded engines that are designed for only one flight which we’ll use on future Artemis missions.
Similar to the Shuttle, the SLS uses two Solid Rocket Boosters. On the Shuttle they were made up of four segments, but the SLS SRBs will have five segments to provide more power. <Point to SRB segment lines>
At the other end of the vehicle we have Orion. Several times I’ve mentioned the Orion crew module. Actually, the Orion spacecraft is made up of two parts. The top part where the crew stays, which looks shiny in this view, is called the Crew Module. Below that, connected to the Crew Module, is the European Service Module which is provided by the European Space Agency (ESA).
This Service Module provides in-space propulsion, batteries to store the power from its solar array wings, and life support such as oxygen, water, and temperature control.
Attached to the Crew Module is a white aerodynamic fairing that protects the module from turbulence  and atmospheric heating during launch.
Also attached to the Crew Module is the Launch Abort System. <Point to LAS> If anything bad happens during launch or ascent we can fire this small rocket and pull the crew away from the rest of the vehicle in about 3 seconds. Once it’s far enough away, the parachutes will deploy and the crew will safely splash down into the ocean.
Below Orion is the Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (which we call ‘ICPS’ because it’s much easier to say). <Point to ICPS> This is the upper stage that will send Orion to the Moon. When it’s finished firing it will separate and Orion will head to the Moon by itself.   [ICPS is built by Aerojet Rocketdyne and uses one RL-10 engine fueled by liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen.]
To keep program costs down everything in SLS is expendable except Orion. Re-using hardware is cost-effective if you’re flying multiple times a year. If you’re reserving some fuel to fly your first stage booster back to Earth, like SpaceX does, then you can’t carry as much payload.
SLS is designed to fly to the Moon just once a year so we use all our fuel to get maximum performance, then we separate from the burned-out elements and dispose of them.
Just before the launch countdown reaches zero the four Core Stage engines are started. When all the engines are up and running we ignite the SRBs. Once the SRBs are started we can’t stop them, sort of like bottle rockets, so we make sure all four engines are up and running before we fire the SRBs.
After two minutes the SRBs are burned out so they separate and drop into the ocean and sink, unlike in the Shuttle program where they were recovered and re-used multiple times.
A minute later the Launch Abort System rocket and the fairings on Orion are released and they fall away.
The four engines on the Core Stage burn for another 6 1/2 minutes until they run out of fuel. The Core Stage will then separate from ICPS and Orion, drop back into the atmosphere, break apart and burn up just like the External Tank did on the Shuttle. What doesn’t burn up will drop into the ocean and sink to the bottom. This is how used rocket segments have been disposed of for many years.
This leaves ICPS and Orion in Low Earth Orbit. At the appropriate time ICPS fires, then separates, sending Orion to the Moon. 
This will be the general sequence for all SLS launches. The SLS can carry crew, cargo and sometimes it will even carry both.

{{< slide n="8" img="slides/slide-008.webp" >}}
What I’m presenting here are the current plans for the Artemis program. As with any NASA program things may change over time, but this is the plan as of today.
We’re starting with what we call ‘Block 1’, a smaller version of the SLS, which will grow to Block 1B and eventually to Block 2.
The rocket I’ve already described for you is the Block 1 vehicle.  <Point to Block 1>
The ICPS in Block 1, the upper stage that sends Orion to the Moon, has only one engine, model RL10, which is good enough for the first three Artemis missions. But we need more power to lift the payloads that are planned for future missions.
We’ve already started developing its replacement, the Exploration Upper Stage (EUS).
The EUS will have four improved RL10 engines giving it nearly 4 times as much thrust as the current ICPS.
That will create the Block 1B vehicle.   <Point to Block 1B>
Then eventually we will replace the Solid Rocket Boosters with more powerful boosters and we’ll use whatever the latest technology is at that time.
That will be the Block 2 vehicle.   <Point to Block 2>
It remains to be seen how far the SLS will be allowed to evolve.

Artemis I launched without a crew on November 16, 2022, at 12:47 AM Central time [06:47 UTC].
Just so you know, we didn’t launch at night just to make a big splash in the sky. The launch window is determined by celestial mechanics. That is, it depends on the location of the Sun, the Moon and the Earth at any given time.
The Orion Crew Module operates on batteries starting at launch. The batteries are good for 90 minutes and once it’s in orbit the solar array wings deploy as soon as possible to start recharging the batteries.
The flight path must not take it through the path of an eclipse—that it, the shadow of the Moon—for more than 90 minutes. The position of the Sun, Moon and Earth all determine where the shadow of the Moon will be, and thus, when the rocket should launch.

{{< slide n="15" img="slides/slide-015.webp" >}}
After a 4-day return trip, Orion re-entered Earth’s atmosphere going about 25,000 mph where it was exposed to 5000 degree heating.
The atmosphere gradually slowed it to 325 mph, which is when the parachutes started deploying.
Eight parachutes gradually slowed it down until, at 130 mph, these three main chutes deployed and slowed it to a splashdown speed of 17 mph.
The orange airbags on top, in the center image, are called the Crew Module Uprighting System. When the crew module lands in the water it is very stable in the position you see, which we call ‘Stable 1’. Unfortunately, it’s also very stable in another position that we call ‘Stable 2’, which is completely upside down. If it’s in Stable 2 it’s pretty hard to get your crew out so we use the orange airbags to flip it over if necessary, or to keep it from going into Stable 2 if the seas get rough.
The picture on the right shows a U.S. Navy well-deck ship. Divers attached a cable to Orion after it splashed down. Then they winched it into a specially designed cradle inside the ship’s well deck, the door was closed, the water was pumped out and the ship returned to port. Orion is white and the airbags are yellow in this picture because it was taken during recovery testing earlier in the year.

Artemis II is a crewed mission currently planned for April 2026 but could launch as early as February 2 [as of 25Sep25].
The four astronauts have already been chosen. They are Commander Reid Wiseman, Pilot Victor Glover, Mission Specialists Christina Koch [pronounced ‘cook’] and Canadian Space Agency (CSA) astronaut Jeremy Hansen.
The goals of Artemis II are:
Check out proximity operations in Earth orbit to assess Orion’s handling qualities and related hardware and software. That is, Orion will maneuver around the ICPS but won’t dock with it.
Secondly, we want to verify all spacecraft systems, such as the newly-installed life support systems, and to make sure they operate as designed with crew aboard in the actual environment of deep space.
Artemis II will take about four days to reach the Moon.
Once there, it will swing about 6000 miles beyond the Moon without going into lunar orbit, use the Moon's gravity to slingshot back toward Earth, then take four days to return to Earth on a 'free-return trajectory’.
This means there will be no significant on-board rocket firings needed because this trajectory will use Earth’s gravity to pull Orion home.
By using this trajectory we can still get our astronauts home if there are problems with the on-board propulsion system.

Artemis III, which will include landing astronauts on the Moon, is currently planned for mid-2027 [as of Jan25]. However, that date may slip if SpaceX doesn’t develop their Starship and in-space fuel depot in time.
The primary goal of Artemis III is to explore the region near the lunar South Pole.
It will have a crew of four, and the two that land on the Moon will be the first woman and first person of color on the surface of the Moon.
Since Orion was never designed to land on the moon, NASA awarded SpaceX the contract to provide a lunar lander for Artemis III with their Starship, which is currently being tested.
Before Starship heads to the moon it will have to refuel at a fuel depot in Low Earth Orbit that SpaceX also has yet to develop.
Once operational, this orbiting fuel depot will need to be filled by 8 to 15 Starship flights launched solely to carry fuel to the depot.
Once the depot has been filled, the Starship that's heading to the moon will dock with it and take on-board all that fuel.
Even then, it will have only enough fuel to get to the moon, not enough to return to Earth.
Once this system is operational, SpaceX will send an uncrewed Starship to land on the Moon to prove it works and is safe.
If this is successful, eventually another Starship will be sent to the Moon to be in orbit waiting for Orion when it arrives on Artemis III.
Once Orion reaches the Moon it will dock with Starship, two astronauts will stay on-board Orion and two astronauts will fly Starship down to land on the Moon.
During their time on the Moon the astronauts will do scientific work inside Starship and conduct moonwalks to take pictures and video, survey geology, gather samples, and collect other data.
After 6 1/2 days, which is more than twice the longest continuous time that humans have spent on the Moon, they will use Starship to return to orbit and dock with Orion, then all four astronauts will return to Earth in Orion.
This version of Starship is not designed to return to Earth. Instead, it will be sent to orbit the sun where it will eventually fall into the sun and burn up. [heliocentric disposal orbit]
The sun is over a million times the size of the Earth and it won't even know the Starship is there.

Artemis IV is currently planned for 2028 or later and will involve a lunar landing and also start assembling the lunar Gateway, a mini space station that will orbit the moon.
The payload for Artemis IV will be four astronauts in Orion as well as a habitation module provided by the European Space Agency (ESA).
Once at the moon, Orion and and the hab module will dock with the other Gateway components that had been launched previously.
Two astronauts will remain on Gateway and two astronauts will land on the Moon using Starship.
Again they will stay for 6 1/2 days.
This is the second time that I’ve mentioned 6 1/2 days. This is how long it takes Orion to make a long elliptical orbit around the Moon before Starship can dock with it.
As before, the lunar crew will return from the Moon in Starship, dock with Gateway, the astronauts will get into Orion and all four will return to Earth.
Gateway will stay in orbit around the Moon and Starship will likely be sent to the Sun again.
Many things need to be finished before Artemis IV can launch, and SpaceX has a lot of work to do before they'll be able to get Starship to the moon.

{{< slide n="21" img="slides/slide-021.webp" >}}
The lunar Gateway is like a mini-space-station, about 1/6 the size of the International Space Station. It’s a multi-purpose human-tended outpost orbiting the Moon that will support long-term human return to the lunar surface.
With a 15-year design life we will use it to develop technologies to support future Moon exploration and act as a staging point for deep space exploration to Mars.
It has enough space for 4 astronauts to live and work for up to 30 days and it can operate autonomously when crew are not on board for up to 3 years at a time.
This will allow us to run continuous deep space science and technology investigations to study human health, life sciences, cosmic rays, radiation, space weather and much more.
The U.S. elements are already in development with commercial partners and we have partnerships in place with the European, Canadian and Japanese space agencies.
As you can imagine, it takes a lot of people to build this hardware. I want to give you an idea of how many companies are involved in Artemis…

Power and Propulsion Element (PPE) will be built by Maxar, a company based in a suburb of Denver, CO. It will provide communications, solar arrays, solar electric [Hall-effect] propulsion and hydrazine thrusters.
Habitation and Logistic Outpost (HALO) will be built by Northrup Grumman and will provide habitable volume, communications, energy storage & distribution and docking ports.
The I-HAB built by the Europeans and Japanese will be the main living quarters for the crew as well as having 4 docking ports.
The ESPRIT Refueler will be built by ESA and will provide fuel delivery, cargo transport, some habitable volume, observation windows, storage space, and docking ports.
Canadarm3 Robotic Arm – Provided by CSA, in exchange for Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen on Artemis II, and eventually another Canadian astronaut on Gateway
HLS landers – Provided by Starship & The National Team (Blue Origin/Lockheed/Northrup Grumman/Draper)
Logistic modules provided by SpaceX and Japanese Space Agency (HTV)

Shown here are the ten principles of the Artemis Accords.
The Artemis Accords is a non-binding multilateral agreement between the United States government and other world governments participating in the Artemis program.
The Accords establish a framework for cooperation among these nations that cover the civil exploration and peaceful use of the Moon, Mars, comets and asteroids.
Even countries who aren’t involved in Artemis can sign the Accords to show they are committed to the responsible exploration of space.
